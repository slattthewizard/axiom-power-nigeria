A pump order that goes wrong usually starts with the wrong classification, not the wrong brand. A maintenance manager replaces a failed unit with something close in flange size and horsepower, the new pump runs, and three months later it is cavitating, leaking at the seal, or simply not moving enough fluid at the pressure the process needs. Getting the types of pumps right before you specify one saves that second failure.

Every pump used on a Nigerian industrial site falls into one of two broad families: centrifugal pumps, which use a spinning impeller to add kinetic energy to the fluid, and positive displacement pumps, which move a fixed volume of fluid with each revolution or stroke. Within each family there are several configurations, and each one suits a different combination of flow, pressure, viscosity and solids content. This guide sets out the pump classification a plant engineer actually needs, matches common types to typical plant duties such as boiler feed, cooling water and fuel transfer, and notes what tends to fail first on each.

## The Two Broad Families: Centrifugal and Positive Displacement

The distinction that matters most on a plant is how each family responds to a change in system pressure. A centrifugal pump has a fixed speed-flow-head curve: as the resistance in the pipework rises (a partly closed valve, a fouled filter, a higher static lift), the flow it delivers falls. A positive displacement pump behaves almost the opposite way. It delivers close to the same volume per revolution or stroke regardless of pressure, up to the limit of the driver and the relief valve protecting the system. Push against a closed valve with a positive displacement pump and pressure climbs rapidly rather than flow simply dropping off, which is why every PD installation needs a relief valve routed back to suction or to tank.

That single difference explains most of the specification mistakes seen on site: a centrifugal pump throttled hard for flow control wastes energy and can run into an unstable part of its curve, while a positive displacement pump throttled the same way can overpressure the piping in seconds.

## Centrifugal Pump Configurations on a Typical Plant

Centrifugal pumps cover the majority of general service duty because they are simple, tolerant of a range of flows, and cheap to maintain relative to the volume they move. The configurations you will meet on a Nigerian industrial site include:

- End suction pumps, the most common configuration for general water, cooling and low-viscosity process service at moderate flow and head, mounted close-coupled or with a separate bearing frame.
- Split case pumps, built with a horizontally split casing so the rotating element lifts out without disturbing the pipework, favoured for higher flow duties such as plant-wide cooling water or fire water where downtime for maintenance has to be short.
- Multistage pumps, which stack several impellers on one shaft to build head in steps, used wherever the duty needs pressure well beyond what a single impeller can generate, such as boiler feed or high-rise water supply.
- Vertical turbine pumps, suspended down a borehole or a wet sump with the driver at surface level, used for well water supply and deep sump drainage.
- Submersible pumps, with the motor sealed and mounted below the liquid level, used in sumps, effluent pits and boreholes where a dry-mounted pump is impractical or where flooding risk rules it out.

All of these share the same vulnerability: they depend on a steady supply of liquid at the suction, called adequate available suction head, and they are not designed to run dry. Running a centrifugal pump against a closed or blocked suction, even briefly, can score the wear rings, overheat the seal faces and cook the bearings.

## Positive Displacement Pump Configurations on a Typical Plant

Positive displacement pumps are the choice once the duty involves high viscosity, precise metering, or pressure well beyond what a centrifugal stage can economically produce. The configurations most plants will hold somewhere on site are:

- Gear pumps, using two meshing gears to move fluid through the tooth spaces, common for lubricating oil circulation, hydraulic power packs and light fuel transfer.
- Screw pumps, using one or more intermeshing helical rotors, chosen where flow needs to be smooth with low pulsation, including some viscous fuel and oil duties.
- Lobe pumps, gentler on the product than gears because the lobes do not contact each other, used where the fluid carries some solids or must not be sheared.
- Diaphragm pumps, moving fluid by flexing a membrane rather than through close-tolerance rotating parts, chosen for chemical dosing, corrosive fluids and duties where any leakage past a seal is unacceptable.
- Reciprocating plunger pumps, generating the highest pressures of any configuration on this list through a piston or plunger stroke, used for high-pressure washing, hydrotesting and some dosing duties.
- Progressive cavity pumps, using a single helical rotor turning inside a stator, well suited to sludges, slurries and viscous or shear-sensitive fluids at steady, low-pulsation flow.

Because a positive displacement pump's output tracks its speed rather than the pressure it sees, these configurations are the natural choice wherever the process needs accurate, repeatable flow: chemical dosing, fuel transfer where volume matters, and metering duties generally.

## Centrifugal vs Positive Displacement Pump: Matching Type to Duty

The table below sets out the practical differences that should drive a specification decision, rather than habit or what was fitted last time.

| Consideration | Centrifugal pump | Positive displacement pump |
|---|---|---|
| Viscosity | Best with thin, water-like fluids; efficiency drops sharply as viscosity rises | Handles viscous and sticky fluids well; some configurations improve with moderate viscosity |
| Solids and abrasives | Tolerates light solids loading in a slurry-rated design, but close clearances wear | Lobe and progressive cavity types handle solids and slurries well; gear and screw types wear quickly on abrasives |
| Flow response to pressure change | Flow falls as system resistance rises | Flow stays close to constant regardless of pressure, up to the relief valve setting |
| Flow control method | Throttle a downstream valve or vary speed with a drive | Vary speed or stroke; never throttle downstream without a relief valve fitted |
| Typical duty fit | High or moderate flow, low to moderate viscosity, continuous running | Lower flow, precise metering, high pressure, or fluids too thick or shear-sensitive for an impeller |
| Dry running tolerance | Poor; can damage seal faces and bearings within seconds | Some configurations (gear, screw) also suffer without lubricating film from the fluid |

## Typical Pump Duties on a Nigerian Industrial Plant

The duty tends to select the pump family before anyone opens a catalogue. Boiler feed duty needs high pressure at moderate flow with clean, treated water, which is why multistage centrifugal pumps dominate that service. Cooling water and general process water circulation, by contrast, need high flow at comparatively low head, and end suction or split case centrifugal pumps handle that economically at scale. Fuel transfer from a storage tank to a generator or turbine day tank favours a gear or screw pump, since diesel and heavier fuel oils are more viscous than water and the transfer needs to be self-priming and reasonably tolerant of a partly empty suction line. Process duties vary by fluid: a low-viscosity process stream stays centrifugal territory, while a chemical dosing line, a slurry, or a thick effluent stream moves into diaphragm or progressive cavity territory. Fire water service almost always runs on split case or vertical turbine centrifugal pumps, sized to a fixed rated duty point and periodically flow-tested rather than adjusted for day-to-day convenience.

Getting this wrong is not a paperwork problem. A plant that specifies a centrifugal pump for a duty that really needed positive displacement metering will chase flow instability and seal failures indefinitely; a plant that puts a positive displacement pump on a duty better served by a centrifugal machine pays for it in maintenance cost and complexity it does not need. ## What Fails First on Each Pump Type

On centrifugal pumps, the mechanical seal is usually the first thing to show trouble, whether from dry running, misalignment, or a flush plan that does not suit the fluid; the failure modes and how to read a failed seal are covered in /blog/mechanical-seal-failure/. Bearings follow close behind, often as a secondary effect of the same misalignment or of cavitation vibration rather than simple wear-out. Impellers and wear rings erode over time from solids or cavitation damage, showing up as a slow loss of head and flow rather than a sudden stop. On positive displacement pumps, gear and lobe units wear at the close-tolerance faces when the fluid carries abrasive contamination; diaphragm pumps fail by rupture, usually from chemical incompatibility, fatigue, or an unprotected pressure spike; reciprocating plunger pumps wear at the valves and packing first; progressive cavity pumps wear at the rotor and stator interface, particularly if the fluid runs dry through the pump even briefly. Any inspection, seal change, alignment check or coupling work on a pump train needs the driver electrically isolated under lockout/tagout with a permit to work in place; this is not a job to carry out with the machine live.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between centrifugal and positive displacement pumps?

A centrifugal pump uses a spinning impeller to add energy to the fluid, and its flow output falls as system pressure rises. A positive displacement pump moves a fixed volume with each revolution or stroke, so its flow stays close to constant regardless of pressure, which is why it always needs relief valve protection.

### Which pump type suits viscous fluids like diesel or lubricating oil?

Positive displacement configurations, particularly gear and screw pumps, handle viscous fluids far better than centrifugal pumps, whose efficiency and flow drop sharply once viscosity rises above water-like levels. Fuel transfer and lube oil circulation duties are almost always positive displacement for this reason.

### Can a centrifugal pump be allowed to run dry?

No. Centrifugal pumps rely on the pumped liquid for cooling and lubrication at the seal faces and, in many designs, at the bearings, so running dry even briefly can cause rapid seal and bearing damage. Adequate suction supply and a reliable low-level or dry-run protection is part of any correct installation.

### How do I know which type of pump my plant duty actually needs?

Start from the operating point: required flow and head or pressure, the fluid's viscosity and solids content, and whether the duty needs precise metering. Matching that operating point against a genuine pump curve, rather than replacing on flange size alone, is the reliable way to land on the right family and configuration.

### What is the most common reason industrial pumps fail early on Nigerian sites?

Dry running or loss of suction supply, contamination in the fluid causing abrasive wear, and misalignment between pump and driver account for most early failures across both pump families. Each of these is preventable with routine checks rather than a design change.
