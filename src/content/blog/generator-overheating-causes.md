A set that trips on high coolant temperature at 2 pm on a hot Tuesday, runs fine after a half-hour rest and trips again an hour later is telling you something specific. The engine isn't broken yet. It's losing a fight between the heat it makes and the heat it can get rid of, and the margin has shrunk until a hot afternoon tips it over.

Most generator overheating causes are boring. A radiator choked with dust, a room that recirculates its own exhaust air, a belt that's slipping, a coolant mix nobody has checked in two years. Replacing the radiator or pulling the head are the last things to try, not the first. This article puts the causes in the order we'd check them on site, cheapest first, and says where each check stops being safe to do casually.

One rule before anything else. Never open the radiator cap or the expansion tank cap on a hot engine. The system runs under pressure, and the coolant sits well above its normal boiling point at atmospheric pressure. The moment you release the cap it flashes to steam and sprays. Wait until the engine has been stopped long enough that the upper hose can be squeezed by hand, and even then crack the cap slowly with a cloth over it.

## What the engine is actually doing when it overheats

A diesel engine converts roughly a third of its fuel energy into useful work. A large part of the rest leaves through the exhaust, and a big share goes into the coolant. On a loaded set the radiator is rejecting that heat continuously, and it only works if three things hold: enough coolant is circulating, enough air is being pulled through the core, and that air is cooler than the coolant.

Break any one of those and the temperature climbs until the controller alarms or shuts the set down. Typical alarm and shutdown settings sit somewhere in the range of the mid-90s to just over 100 degrees Celsius, but they vary by engine make and by how the controller was programmed, so read the actual setpoints rather than assuming. If you're unsure what your panel is reporting, our guide to [Deep Sea controller alarms](/blog/deep-sea-controller-alarms/) explains how the warning and shutdown stages differ.

A useful first question is when it happens. Overheating that appears only at full load or only in the afternoon points at capacity, meaning airflow or radiator condition. Overheating that appears soon after start-up at any load points at circulation, meaning the thermostat, pump or coolant level. Overheating that comes on suddenly after months of being fine points at something that has just failed, such as a belt or a hose.

## Step 1: Look at the room, the louvres and the radiator face

These checks cost nothing and need no tools. Do them first.

Start with the radiator face. In Nigeria, especially in the north during harmattan, the fins pick up a fine dust that mixes with oil mist from the engine breather and the fan hub and turns into a grey felt. It looks like a light coating but it blocks airflow badly. In the south the usual culprit is airborne grit from roads and construction, plus lint, insects and plant material. Shine a torch through the core from behind. If you can't see light through most of it, it's fouled.

Then the louvres and the room. A generator room needs a large free opening for intake air, and a path for the hot air leaving the radiator to get out of the building and stay out. Check whether the inlet louvres are blocked by a stack of pallets, a new wall, a vendor's shed, or bird nests. Check whether the discharge duct is intact and whether anyone has closed the outlet louvres or put a door in the way.

Hot air recirculation is the cause people miss. If the radiator discharges into a room, or into a short duct that opens beside the air intake, the fan pulls its own hot exhaust back through the core. The set then runs fine in the morning and overheats in the afternoon because ambient air is higher and the loop is feeding itself. A quick test is to stand at the intake louvre with a thermometer while the set is loaded and compare it with outside air in the shade. A large difference means recirculation.

This is also a good moment to read the [generator maintenance checklist](/blog/generator-maintenance-checklist/) for the routine radiator and room checks, because most of what you've just done should be on a weekly or monthly list already.

## Step 2: Coolant level, mix and condition

Next, the coolant. Check the level in the expansion tank with the engine cold, not the radiator cap. Low level means air in the system, a leak, or coolant that's been lost through a failing head gasket or cap.

Look for the obvious leaks first: hose clamps, the water pump weep hole, the bottom of the radiator, drain taps, and the heat exchanger or oil cooler seams. A dry white or green crust on a fitting is dried coolant. If the level keeps dropping with no external leak, suspect the head gasket, and read our notes on [head gasket failure in generators](/blog/head-gasket-failure-generator/) before running the set any further, because a set that keeps overheating on a damaged gasket will warp the head.

Then check the mix. Most engines call for a glycol and water mix, often around half and half, with corrosion inhibitor in it. Too much water gives poor corrosion protection and lets scale form inside the engine. Too much glycol carries heat away less well than water, which surprises people who think more antifreeze means better protection. Topping up with plain tap water for years, which is common, leaves scale in the block and the radiator tubes. Scale insulates, and it can raise running temperature on its own.

Look at the coolant colour. Rusty brown, an oily film on top, or sludge in the tank tells you the inhibitor is spent or oil is getting in from somewhere. Oil in the coolant means an oil cooler or head gasket problem, and that isn't a flush-and-carry-on situation.

## Step 3: Fan belt, fan and shroud

With the set stopped, isolated and locked out under a permit to work, inspect the belts. A slipping fan belt reduces airflow quietly. You'll see glazing on the belt sides, cracking, fraying, or a belt that deflects far more than the manual allows. A squeal on a sudden load step is a giveaway. On sets with a driven fan, a worn tensioner or a loose pulley does the same job.

Check the fan itself for cracked or bent blades, and check that the shroud is intact. The shroud forces the fan to pull air through the core rather than around it. A missing or damaged shroud can cost a surprising amount of cooling, and it's an easy repair. A fan fitted the wrong way round, after a rebuild, will blow when it should suck and the set will overheat on the first long run.

Never reach into a fan area until the engine can't start. Automatic start sets are especially dangerous, so isolate the starting battery and lock out the AMF controller before your hands go anywhere near the blades.

## Step 4: Overload and load pattern

Now look at the load. A set that's regularly pushed above its rating makes more heat than the cooling system was designed to remove. Check the current on each phase and compare with the nameplate rating, then check the kVA and kW readings on the controller. Remember that kW is what loads the engine, so if you need to convert, see our explanation of [kVA to kW conversion](/blog/kva-to-kw-conversion/).

Two more load points. A badly unbalanced three-phase load can overload one winding while the engine looks fine, and a set running on standby rating continuously is working outside what that rating allows. If a plant has grown since the set was bought, the generator may simply be too small for the duty now, and no amount of cleaning will fix that.

Take a hypothetical 500 kVA set that has been fine for years. Add a new compressor and a chiller line and it quietly moves from 60 percent to around 90 percent load. Heat rejection to the coolant rises with load, so a radiator that coped before now has almost no margin, and a slightly fouled core is enough to trip it. The overheating looks like a radiator problem and is really a sizing problem.

## Step 5: Thermostat, water pump and hoses

If the room, radiator, coolant, belts and load all check out, the problem is likely inside the circuit.

A stuck-closed thermostat stops coolant reaching the radiator, so the engine heats up fast, often within minutes of starting, while the radiator top stays cool and the bottom hose stays cool. That's a quick hand test, carried out carefully on a stopped and cooling engine or with a contact thermometer. A thermostat that opens late or only partly gives the same symptom more slowly. Thermostats are cheap and do wear out, and I'd replace a doubtful one rather than argue about it. Don't ever remove the thermostat and run without one as a cure. The coolant then short-circuits the radiator and, on many engines, runs the block hotter, not cooler.

The water pump fails by wearing its impeller, leaking through its seal, or having the impeller slip on its shaft. Signs are coolant weeping from the pump's tell-tale hole, bearing noise, play in the pulley, and overheating that gets worse with speed and load. A collapsed bottom hose, which sucks flat under suction when the engine is running, restricts flow and mimics a bad pump. A radiator cap that no longer holds its rated pressure lowers the boiling point and causes boil-over, and caps are a cheap swap.

Internally blocked radiator tubes, from scale or years of poor coolant, can be found with a temperature gun across the core. A cold patch means a blocked section. By this point you're into radiator cleaning or recoring, and that's a job for a workshop.

## Step 6: Combustion and turbocharger causes

Anything that makes more heat than it should will overload the cooling system. Retarded injection timing, worn or faulty injectors, a blocked air filter, restricted exhaust, or a failing turbocharger all push heat into the coolant and the exhaust. Black smoke with high temperature suggests an air or fuel delivery problem, and our pages on [injector and pump problems](/blog/injector-pump-problems/) cover that side. Honestly, if the cooling system looks healthy and the set still runs hot, it's usually a combustion problem, and you'll want an engineer with the right gauges.

## Alternator winding overheating is a separate problem

Don't mix up engine overheating with generator end overheating. The alternator has its own temperature limits, set by the winding insulation class. Winding temperature sensors, if fitted, alarm separately from the coolant.

Causes include blocked alternator cooling airflow, clogged air intake screens, overload and high current, poor power factor, unbalanced phases, harmonic loading from drives and rectifiers, and failing insulation. A power factor much below the nameplate value increases the current the alternator carries for the same kW. If you suspect winding trouble, don't keep loading it. An insulation resistance test with a megger, done with the set isolated, locked out and covered by a permit to work, tells you more than a temperature reading will. Our guide to [megger insulation testing](/blog/megger-test-insulation-resistance/) covers the method.

## A quick reference table

| What you see | Most likely cause | First check |
|---|---|---|
| Trips only on hot afternoons at high load | Fouled radiator or hot air recirculation | Torch through the core, intake air temperature |
| Overheats minutes after start at any load | Stuck thermostat or low coolant | Expansion tank level, top and bottom hose temperature |
| Gradual creep over weeks | Radiator fouling, scale, weak belt | Radiator face, belt tension |
| Sudden overheating after months of normal running | Broken belt, burst hose, lost coolant | Belt, hoses, level |
| Coolant level falls with no visible leak | Head gasket or oil cooler leak | Oil on coolant, bubbles in the tank |
| Alternator hot, engine temperature normal | Overload, poor airflow, insulation fault | Phase currents, intake screens, insulation test |

## Fixing the root cause, not the alarm

Don't be tempted to raise the shutdown setpoint to stop the nuisance trips. That removes the protection while the cause stays in place, and the next thing that fails will be the head, a liner or the turbocharger bearings. Keep a simple log of coolant temperature against load and ambient on each run, so a slow drift shows up weeks before the alarm does. Cleaning the radiator, the filters and the louvres on a schedule that matches your site's dust, and testing the coolant condition at set intervals, prevents most trips.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What temperature is too hot for a diesel generator?

Most engines run normally somewhere around the high 70s to low 90s degrees Celsius, depending on the thermostat rating and load. Warning alarms commonly start in the mid-90s and shutdowns just above 100, but the exact setpoints depend on the engine and controller, so use the manufacturer's figures. A reading that keeps climbing past the usual running band is a fault, not a normal hot day.

### Can I keep running a generator that shows a high coolant temperature alarm?

Only briefly and only to reduce the load or shut it down in an orderly way. Running through a high temperature alarm can warp the cylinder head, blow the head gasket and score the liners, and those repairs cost far more than a cleaned radiator. If the set is critical, transfer load to another source, let it idle to cool, and find the cause before you restart.

### Why does my generator overheat only at full load?

At full load the engine makes the most heat, so any weakness in the cooling system shows up there first. The usual causes are a partly blocked radiator, a slipping belt, poor room ventilation, or recirculated hot air. It can also mean the set is simply too small for the connected load. Check the airflow and the radiator before you suspect anything inside the engine.

### Is it safe to add water to a hot radiator?

No. Opening the cap on a hot engine releases pressurised coolant as scalding steam, and cold water poured into a hot block can crack it. Let the engine stop and cool until the upper hose can be held by hand, open the cap slowly with a cloth over it, and top up with the correct coolant mix, not plain water.

### Does overheating mean the head gasket has failed?

Not usually. Most overheating comes from fouled radiators, poor airflow, low coolant or a thermostat fault. A head gasket becomes the suspect when the coolant level keeps dropping with no external leak, when there are bubbles or oil in the coolant, or when white smoke appears. Have it pressure tested before anything is dismantled.
