Most generator houses we're asked to look at weren't designed. They were built first, as a block room with a door and a vent, and the set was dropped in afterwards. Then the set trips on high coolant temperature every afternoon, the neighbours complain, and the fuel line runs across a walkway. Fixing a room costs far more than getting it right on paper.

This guide covers generator room design for industrial sets, roughly 100 kVA up to multi-megawatt, in Nigerian conditions: heat, dust, heavy rain and long running hours. It doesn't replace the engine maker's installation manual or a structural and fire design by a registered professional. It's the checklist we wish every client had before the first block was laid.

## Start with the maker's data, not a rule of thumb

Every number that matters comes from the engine and alternator data sheets: heat rejected to coolant, heat radiated to the room, combustion air flow, radiator fan airflow, exhaust gas flow and temperature, allowable intake and exhaust restriction, and the dimensions and weight of the set on its baseframe.

Get those figures in writing for the exact model before you draw anything. We won't quote airflow or noise figures here because they vary by engine, cooling arrangement and site temperature. A set with a remote radiator needs a very different room from one with a set-mounted radiator, even at the same kVA.

Size the set first, because the room follows the set. If the rating and loading aren't settled, read our [generator sizing guide](/blog/generator-sizing-guide/) before you commit to a footprint. Adding a second set later is common, so decide now whether the building can grow, and leave an end wall that can be opened up.

## Airflow: the usual cause of overheating

A running diesel set needs air for three jobs. The engine burns it, the radiator rejects heat into it, and the room has to carry away the heat radiated off the engine and alternator. The radiator fan moves by far the largest volume.

The most reliable layout is simple. Air enters through a louvred opening at the alternator end, flows past the alternator and engine, passes through the radiator, and leaves through a duct from the radiator outlet to an opening in the wall or roof. The duct joins the radiator with a flexible canvas or rubber collar so the set can vibrate without cracking metal.

What decides whether it works:

- Size the inlet louvres from the maker's figure for combustion plus cooling air, using the louvre's free area, not the gross opening. Ask the supplier for free area and pressure drop.
- Keep the discharge duct within the maker's allowable static pressure at the radiator. Long runs, tight bends and fine mesh all add resistance.
- Put intake and discharge on different faces of the building, or far enough apart that hot air can't loop back. Recirculation is why a set runs fine for twenty minutes and then overheats.

In the north, harmattan dust clogs fine louvre mesh and radiator cores quickly. In the south, salt air and humidity attack bare steel. Specify galvanised or aluminium louvres, make them removable, and write the cleaning interval into the [generator maintenance](/generator-maintenance-nigeria/) plan so somebody owns it.

## Exhaust routing, silencer and stack height

Exhaust gas is hot, noisy and carries carbon monoxide. Treat it as a hazard in its own right.

Run it from the turbocharger outlet through a flexible bellows, then the silencer, then rigid pipe out of the building and upward. Support the pipe from the structure, never from the engine. A heavy silencer hanging on a turbo flange cracks housings, and we see it more than we'd like.

The back pressure the engine tolerates is in the data sheet. Every metre of pipe, every bend and the silencer use part of that allowance, so keep runs short and don't reduce the pipe diameter below the maker's size. Lag the pipe inside the room, because bare exhaust adds a lot to the ventilation load. Where it passes through a wall or roof, use a sleeve with clearance and a rain collar, away from anything combustible.

Where the stack ends matters as much as how you get there. Take it above the roofline of nearby buildings, away from the generator house's own air intakes, away from offices and process air, and away from neighbours and the boundary wall if you can. Fit a rain cap or angled cut so water doesn't run into the turbocharger. Give each set its own stack. Joining two into one common stack lets a stopped engine fill with gas from a running one unless the design is very careful.

## Noise and the neighbours

Noise comes from the engine, fan, exhaust and intake. The fan and exhaust are usually the worst, and you can't just wall them off, because they need air to pass.

The usual toolkit is a critical-grade silencer, acoustic louvres or attenuators on the inlet and discharge openings, a heavy envelope (masonry or concrete rather than light sheet), and sealed, gasketed doors. Attenuators cost pressure drop, so tell the louvre designer what the radiator fan can tolerate.

The openings are the weak points. A room with thick walls and a bare louvre is a loud room. Face openings away from the nearest dwellings, and let a wall or earth bund shield the boundary side. What level you must meet at the boundary depends on local rules and your agreement with neighbours. We cover the approach in [generator noise and emission limits](/blog/generator-noise-emission-limits/). Check the current requirement with the regulator rather than working from a number you saw online.

## Fuel: day tank, bunding and separation from the bulk tank

Don't pipe a bulk tank straight into the engine room. The usual arrangement is a bulk tank outside, a transfer pump, and a day tank in or beside the house, sized for how long the set should run between fills.

- Put the day tank inside a bund that can hold its full volume. The bulk tank needs its own bund, sized by whoever designs the tank installation.
- Keep the bulk tank at a sensible distance from the building and any ignition source. How far depends on tank size and the applicable fire and approval rules, so confirm with the authority concerned.
- Fit a high-level alarm or overflow return so the transfer pump can't overfill the day tank.
- Use a flexible, fire-rated hose between the rigid fuel pipe and the engine, with a shut-off valve outside the room that you can reach in an emergency.
- Provide a way to drain water and sediment from the tank bottom. Honestly, most injector and pump trouble we see starts with dirty fuel.

If storing diesel on your site needs an approval, find out early. It affects where the bulk tank can go, which affects the whole layout.

## Plinth, vibration and flexible connections

The set sits on a reinforced concrete plinth raised above finished floor level and isolated from the building slab, so vibration doesn't travel through the structure. A structural engineer designs it for the set's wet weight plus dynamic loads, and the supplier picks anti-vibration mounts for its weight and speed. Don't bolt a big set rigidly to a slab and hope.

Everything that connects to the set must be able to move a little: exhaust bellows, fuel hoses, the radiator duct collar, and flexible conduit or loose cable loops at the alternator terminal box. A rigid pipe on a mounted set is a crack waiting to happen.

## Cable trench, earthing and the electrical side

Run alternator cables to the switchgear in a covered trench that drains, so it doesn't fill in the wet season.

Earthing needs real thought, not a copper rod near the door. The set's neutral, frame and the building need a coordinated arrangement, tested after construction and at intervals. Our article on [industrial generator earthing](/blog/generator-earthing-industrial/) goes through the options.

Anyone working inside the room on live equipment needs isolation, lockout-tagout and a permit to work. Design for that: a labelled isolator within reach, a clear walkway to it, and enough light to read the labels.

## Access, lifting clearance, fire safety and drainage

Think about the day the engine comes apart. Sooner or later a head comes off, a turbocharger changes, a radiator is pulled, or the whole engine is lifted. If the room won't allow it, downtime stretches and the bill climbs.

Leave a workable walkway on every side of the set, not only the front. Provide double doors or a removable wall panel big enough for the largest component. For bigger sets, fit a gantry beam or overhead rail with its safe working load marked on it. Any lift during an overhaul needs a lift plan and a permit to work. Doors should open outwards, and nobody should be able to get trapped.

Fire protection belongs in the design, not the afterthought pile. Which system suits you (extinguishers, fixed suppression, detection and alarms) depends on installation size and on your insurer's and the fire authority's requirements. Ask early, because it changes layout and ventilation. Always provide emergency stops inside and outside the room, non-combustible construction, emergency lighting that works with the set off, and good general lighting for inspections.

Rain here is heavy and sudden. Raise the floor above surrounding ground, slope the yard away from the building, and give the fuel bund a way to be pumped out after rain without letting diesel leave the site. Keep the cable trench from doubling as a drain.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How big should a generator house be?

There's no single ratio. The footprint depends on the set's dimensions, working space on every side, the airflow path, the exhaust, any switchgear in the same room and space for a second set. Start from the maker's installation drawings and add the access and lifting clearance you'll need for overhauls.

### Can the radiator be outside the generator house?

Yes. Many industrial installations use a remote radiator, which moves most of the heat load out of the room and shrinks the ventilation requirement. It adds pipework, a coolant pump and more places to leak, so it needs the engine maker's approval and a proper design.

### Do I need a separate room for the fuel tank?

Not always, but the bulk tank should sit outside the engine room, separated by a distance and containment that suit its size and your local fire and approval requirements. Inside the room, keep only the day tank, in its own bund.

### Why does my generator overheat only in the afternoon?

Usually it's the room, not the engine. Hot discharge air finds its way back to the inlet, the louvres are partly blocked, or the discharge duct is too restrictive. Check the airflow path and radiator core first, and have a qualified engineer inspect the cooling system under isolation and a permit to work.
