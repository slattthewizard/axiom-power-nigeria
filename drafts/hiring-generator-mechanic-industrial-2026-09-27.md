# Generator Mechanic or Service Engineer: Who Should Work on Your Industrial Genset

Every plant manager who has watched a set trip on load has faced the same call: ring the generator mechanic who fixed the last small fault, or bring in someone with instruments and a service history. Both are legitimate options at different points in a genset's life, and the mistake is treating them as interchangeable once the machine is running critical load, feeding a production line, or backing up a facility that cannot tolerate an unplanned outage.

A search for a generator repair company or generator technician near you will return a long list, and the listing tells you almost nothing about whether the person turning up has a multimeter and a set of spanners or a calibrated test set and a fault-finding method. The distinction matters more in Nigerian operating conditions, where heat, dust, long run hours and variable fuel quality push components toward failure faster than an OEM's temperate-climate service manual assumes.

This article sets out where a roadside mechanic genuinely covers the work, where an industrial set needs a service engineer, and what to ask before either one puts a spanner on your machine.

## What a roadside generator mechanic can genuinely handle

For a small standby set running a few hours a week, most of what goes wrong is mechanical and visible: a flat battery, a clogged fuel filter, a slipped belt, a blocked air filter, a coolant leak from a hose clamp. A competent mechanic who works on gensets regularly will find and fix these without difficulty, and paying for an engineer's day rate to change an air filter is not a sensible use of budget.

The boundary sits where the fault stops being purely mechanical. A set that starts, runs, and then trips is giving you a controller alarm, an electrical fault, or a fuel or air delivery problem that needs instrumented diagnosis, not trial and error. Swapping parts until the trip stops costs more in downtime and in parts than a proper fault-finding sequence, and it risks masking a root cause that comes back under load a month later.

## When to move beyond a roadside generator mechanic

An industrial set earns different treatment because the cost of getting it wrong is different. A 500 kVA or larger machine feeding a production line, a data hall, a hospital load, or a telecom site is not a domestic backup set with a bigger engine. It has an AVR and exciter circuit, a programmable controller with dozens of alarm and shutdown parameters, protection relays, and in many cases an automatic transfer switch or synchronising panel that has to interact correctly with the utility supply and with other sets on the same bus.

Move to a qualified generator service engineer once any of the following applies:

- The set carries load that a trip would make expensive, not just inconvenient.
- The fault involves the controller, AVR, protection relays, or paralleling and transfer equipment rather than a mechanical part you can see fail.
- The genset is under a maintenance contract with reporting requirements, since our [generator maintenance contract guide](/blog/generator-maintenance-contract/) sets out what a proper contract should specify.
- You need documented evidence for insurance, warranty, or an internal audit that the work was carried out correctly.
- The set runs continuously or near-continuously as prime power rather than occasional standby duty.

## Diagnostic tools and method: what separates the two

The clearest practical difference between a mechanic and an engineer is what they bring to the job and how they use it. A mechanic's kit is usually a multimeter, basic hand tools, and experience with the common failure patterns on the engines they see most often. That is enough for the mechanical faults described above.

An industrial fault, particularly an intermittent one, needs a sequence: read and log the controller's alarm history before resetting anything, check battery voltage and charging circuit, verify AVR output and residual magnetism, check governor and speed sensor signals, and only then look at the mechanical side if the electrical checks come back clean. Skipping straight to parts replacement, which is the mechanic's default when the fault is not obvious, wastes the one chance to see the fault condition as it actually occurred.

An engineer working an industrial set should also be equipped for insulation resistance testing, load bank testing where the running load is too light or too variable to prove capacity, and vibration or oil condition checks where the maintenance programme calls for them. None of this is exotic equipment. It is standard test gear that a mechanic working from a toolbox on a motorbike will not typically carry.

## Documentation and parts traceability

A repair without a written record is a repair you cannot learn from and cannot prove happened. For an industrial set, ask for a fault report that states what the symptom was, what readings were taken, what was found, what was replaced, and what the post-repair test results showed. This becomes the record you refer to if the same fault recurs, if a warranty claim is disputed, or if an insurer or auditor asks how the plant is maintained.

Parts traceability matters just as much. The market carries a wide range of counterfeit and mismatched components, particularly for filters, belts, sensors, and electronic modules such as AVRs and speed governors. A properly run repair states the part number fitted, whether it is OEM or a stated equivalent, and where it came from. A mechanic working informally will often fit whatever is available locally without recording it, which is acceptable for a low-consequence part and a real risk for anything electronic or safety-related.

## Electrical competence and safety authorisation

Any work that involves opening a control panel, testing insulation resistance, working near a transfer switch, or isolating a set from load touches live electrical systems and, on larger installations, rotating machinery and pressurised cooling systems. This work must be carried out by personnel competent in electrical isolation, lockout and tagout, and working under a permit to work where your site operates one. Ask any technician, whether described as a mechanic or an engineer, what isolation procedure they follow before opening a panel, and do not allow anyone to work on a live control system without proper isolation in place. This guidance is not a substitute for a documented isolation procedure and should not be read as instructions for carrying out live electrical work.

## Roadside mechanic or industrial service engineer: a quick comparison

| Task | Roadside generator mechanic | Industrial service engineer |
|---|---|---|
| Routine service on a small standby set (oil, filters, belts) | Yes | Yes |
| Fault diagnosis on AVR, governor, or controller logic | Rarely | Yes |
| Insulation resistance and megger testing | No | Yes, with recorded values |
| Load bank testing | No | Yes |
| Written fault report with root cause | Rarely | Yes |
| Traceable, matched replacement parts | Sometimes | Yes |
| Formal isolation and permit to work | Informal | Formal |

## What to ask before you hire

Before agreeing to any repair on a set carrying real load, ask the person or company doing the work:

1. What test equipment will you bring, and what will you check before opening the panel?
2. Will I get a written fault report with readings, not just an invoice?
3. What part numbers are you fitting, and are they OEM or a stated equivalent?
4. What is your isolation and permit to work procedure on live equipment?
5. Have you worked on this controller and alternator combination before?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a generator technician the same as a generator mechanic?

The terms are often used loosely and vary by company. What matters is not the title but whether the person has the test equipment, documented method, and electrical competence the fault requires, which you should confirm directly rather than assume from a job title.

### Can a roadside mechanic void my warranty by working on the set?

It depends on the manufacturer's warranty terms and on whether the work involved a covered component. Check the warranty document for any conditions on who may carry out repairs or servicing, and keep a written record of any work done regardless of who performs it.

### How do I find a generator repair company qualified for industrial sets, not just domestic ones?

Ask about the questions listed in this article rather than relying on how a business describes itself online. A company that answers clearly on test equipment, reporting, and isolation procedure is a stronger signal than advertising alone.

### Does a small standby generator need an engineer rather than a mechanic?

Usually not for routine servicing and common mechanical faults. Move to an engineer once the fault involves the controller, AVR, or protection and transfer equipment, or once the set carries load where a trip has a real cost.

### What should be in a generator fault report?

At minimum, the symptom reported, the readings taken before any part was changed, what was found, what was replaced or adjusted with part numbers, and the results of testing after the repair. Keep these reports together so a recurring fault can be tracked over time.
