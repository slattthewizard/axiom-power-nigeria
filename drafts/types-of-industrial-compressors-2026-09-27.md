# Types of Industrial Compressors: How to Pick the Right One for Your Plant

A maintenance manager asked to spec a new compressor usually starts by searching the types of compressor available, then finds pages written for hobbyists buying a shop unit for a garage. None of that helps when the job is sizing plant air for a factory, instrument air for a control system, process gas for a gas plant, or refrigerant duty for a cold store. The classification that actually matters on an industrial site splits into two families: positive displacement and dynamic. Everything else, reciprocating, rotary screw, vane, scroll, centrifugal, axial, is a subtype of one of those two.

This guide sets out what each type does, where it earns its place in a Nigerian plant, and what it costs you in maintenance hours rather than naira. Sizing and buying decisions come down to duty cycle, flow, pressure and air or gas quality, not brand preference.

## The Two Families Behind Every Type of Compressor

Positive displacement machines trap a fixed volume of gas in a chamber, physically shrink that volume, and push the compressed gas out. Reciprocating, rotary screw, rotary vane and scroll compressors all work this way. Flow stays roughly constant regardless of downstream pressure, within the machine's rated limits, which is why positive displacement units dominate compressed air and process gas duties where pressure swings with demand.

Dynamic machines add energy by accelerating the gas with a rotating impeller or set of blades, then convert that velocity into pressure across a diffuser or set of stationary vanes. Centrifugal and axial compressors are dynamic. Their output flow falls as discharge pressure rises, following a performance curve rather than a fixed displacement, which makes them efficient at high, steady volumes but unforgiving of a system that swings around on demand.

Knowing which family a machine belongs to tells you how it will behave when your plant's demand changes, which is usually the first thing that goes wrong when the wrong type gets specified.

## Reciprocating Compressors

A reciprocating compressor uses a piston moving in a cylinder, with suction and discharge valves opening and closing each stroke. It is the oldest and, cylinder for cylinder, the most tolerant of high pressure ratios of any of the common types. Single-stage units suit modest pressures; multi-stage units with intercooling between stages handle high-pressure duties such as gas injection or specialised process gas service.

On Nigerian sites, reciprocating compressors turn up most often on process gas duty (associated gas handling, gas lift, gas-to-power fuel gas boosting) and on smaller, intermittent plant air jobs. The moving parts, valves, rings, packing on the rod for a non-lubricated design, wear steadily and need scheduled attention. Pulsation and vibration are part of the machine's nature, so foundations, piping supports and dampeners need to be right from installation, not bolted on afterward.

## Rotary Screw Compressors: Oil-Injected and Oil-Free

Rotary screw compressors compress gas between two meshing helical rotors turning in opposite directions. They run smoothly, without the pulsation of a reciprocating machine, and dominate continuous-duty plant air and instrument air across Nigerian factories, hospitals and telecom sites because they tolerate long run hours with less operator attention.

Oil-injected screw compressors use lubricant in the compression chamber for sealing and cooling, then separate it out downstream. They are the standard choice for general plant air. Oil-free screw compressors compress dry, with no lubricant in contact with the air stream, at a higher purchase cost and typically a higher energy cost per unit of air delivered. Oil-free is the correct choice wherever the compressed air contacts a product, a patient, or sensitive instrumentation, since even trace oil carryover is unacceptable in those duties. Screw compressors are sensitive to inlet air quality: dust ingestion and inadequate filtration shorten airend and bearing life, which matters on sites running through Nigeria's dry-season dust load. ## Rotary Vane and Scroll Compressors

Rotary vane compressors use sliding vanes in an eccentric rotor to trap and compress gas pockets against the housing wall. They are compact, run at lower speeds than screw units, and turn up in smaller plant air and vacuum applications, plus specialised low-pressure process duties.

Scroll compressors compress by trapping gas between a fixed and an orbiting spiral element. They run oil-free by design in most models, quietly, and suit smaller, cleaner-air applications: laboratory air, medical air, dental and light instrument air where flow demand is modest. Neither type competes with screw or reciprocating machines at higher flows or pressures, and both fall outside the mainstream of heavy industrial plant air, but they are worth knowing when a facility manager is specifying a smaller, quality-sensitive air system alongside the main plant.

## Centrifugal Compressors

A centrifugal compressor accelerates gas radially outward through a rotating impeller, then recovers that velocity as pressure in a diffuser. Multi-stage centrifugal machines, with several impellers in series, reach the high, continuous flows that a single positive displacement machine cannot deliver economically.

Centrifugal compressors are the default for large, continuous plant air systems in bigger factories, for refrigeration compression at scale, and for process gas duty in oil and gas facilities and gas-to-power plants where a steady, high-volume flow runs for extended periods without much variation. They are efficient at their design point but need surge protection: run one below its minimum stable flow and the machine can surge, a rapid flow reversal that damages the impeller and bearings if left unmanaged. Surge margin, anti-surge control and inlet guide vane response are the things a plant engineer should be asking about before a centrifugal machine goes on order. ## Axial Compressors

Axial compressors move gas parallel to the shaft, through alternating rows of rotating and stationary blades, each row adding a small pressure rise. They achieve the highest efficiency of any compressor type at very high, steady flows, which is why axial compressors form the front end of most industrial gas turbines rather than standing alone as plant air or process gas machines on their own.

Outside gas turbine applications, axial compressors are rare on a typical Nigerian industrial or oil and gas site. A plant engineer is more likely to meet one inside a turbine package than as a standalone unit, so specifying one directly is unusual unless the application is genuinely at that scale.

## Matching Compressor Type to Duty

| Compressor type | Typical plant duty | Flow character | Pressure character | Air/gas quality fit | Maintenance burden |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reciprocating | Process gas, intermittent plant air, high-pressure boosting | Low to moderate | High, multi-stage capable | Handles wet or dirty gas with the right design | High: valves, rings, packing need regular attention |
| Rotary screw (oil-injected) | Continuous plant air | Moderate to high | Moderate | General plant air, needs good inlet filtration | Moderate: airend, filters, separators |
| Rotary screw (oil-free) | Instrument air, food, pharma, medical air | Moderate to high | Moderate | Oil-free duties | Moderate to high: tighter tolerances, specialist parts |
| Vane / scroll | Small plant air, lab/medical air, vacuum | Low | Low to moderate | Clean, quality-sensitive air | Low to moderate |
| Centrifugal | Large continuous plant air, refrigeration, process gas | High | Moderate | Continuous clean or process gas with treatment | Moderate: surge control, bearings, seals |
| Axial | Gas turbine inlet air, very large continuous flow | Very high | Low to moderate per stage | Filtered inlet air | Specialist: usually maintained as part of the turbine package |

The pattern that matters for buying decisions: positive displacement types (reciprocating, screw, vane, scroll) hold flow steady as pressure varies and suit duties where demand swings through the day. Dynamic types (centrifugal, axial) hold efficiency at a steady operating point and suit large, continuous loads. Picking the wrong family for your demand profile is a more expensive mistake than picking the wrong brand within the right family.

## What Actually Drives the Choice on Your Site

Four questions settle most compressor selection decisions before brand or price ever comes into it:

- Flow and pressure required, and how much both vary through a shift, a day and a season.
- Duty cycle: continuous three-shift running against short, intermittent bursts.
- Air or gas quality needed downstream, since an oil-free requirement rules out oil-injected screw and most reciprocating designs outright.
- Available utilities and space: cooling water or air, electrical supply capacity, foundation and ductwork constraints.

Purchase price is a real factor but it is only part of total cost of ownership. Energy consumption over the machine's service life, and specifically running an oversized compressor lightly loaded, usually dwarfs the purchase price difference between compressor families over a few years of operation. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the main difference between positive displacement and dynamic compressors?

A positive displacement compressor traps and physically shrinks a fixed volume of gas, so its flow stays roughly constant even as discharge pressure changes. A dynamic compressor accelerates gas with a rotating impeller and converts velocity into pressure, so its flow output falls as pressure rises, following a performance curve rather than a fixed displacement per cycle.

### Should a factory choose a reciprocating or a screw compressor for plant air?

Screw compressors generally suit continuous, steady plant air demand better because they run smoothly with less vibration and tolerate long duty cycles well. Reciprocating compressors still have a place where pressure requirements are high or where the load is intermittent rather than continuous, and multi-stage reciprocating units remain common on high-pressure process gas duty.

### When does a plant need an oil-free compressor rather than an oil-injected one?

Oil-free is required wherever compressed air can contact a product, a patient or sensitive instrumentation and any trace of oil carryover is unacceptable, such as food processing, pharmaceutical, medical and some electronics or laboratory air systems. General industrial plant air without that contact risk usually runs on oil-injected screw compressors at a lower purchase and energy cost.

### Why do centrifugal compressors need surge protection?

Running a centrifugal compressor below its minimum stable flow can cause surge, a rapid, unstable reversal of flow through the machine that produces damaging pressure pulses and mechanical loads on the impeller, seals and bearings. Anti-surge control systems and inlet guide vanes manage flow to keep the machine above that minimum stable point across its operating range.

### Are axial compressors used as standalone plant compressors in Nigeria?

Rarely. Axial compressors deliver very high, steady flow efficiently but are almost always built into industrial gas turbines as the turbine's own inlet compression stage rather than sold as standalone plant air or process gas units, so a maintenance manager is more likely to encounter one inside a turbine package than on an air compressor specification sheet.
