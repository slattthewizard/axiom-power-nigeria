# Captive Power Plant Options for a Nigerian Factory: Diesel, Gas, Turbine or Hybrid

A factory that has outgrown standby diesel sets but cannot get enough hours of reliable supply from the grid eventually asks the same question: should we build our own captive power plant. The term covers several different pieces of equipment, from a bank of diesel gensets to a gas turbine with a steam bottoming cycle, and just as many ways to own and run it. Getting the wrong combination means paying for fuel flexibility, redundancy or capacity the plant never actually uses.

This is not a one-size answer. The right captive power plant for a cement line running three shifts is not the right one for a food factory that only needs full power for two shifts and a lighter load overnight. Load profile, fuel access, available space and how much operating risk the factory wants to carry all point toward a different mix of equipment and a different ownership model.

The sections below set out the main technology options, how to weigh them against your own load and fuel situation, and the operating models used to run the plant once it is built. For the regulatory side of self-generation in Nigeria, including permits and thresholds, see our guide to [captive power permits](/blog/captive-power-permit-nigeria/).

## What counts as a captive power plant

A captive power plant is generation built to supply one facility or a defined group of facilities under common ownership, rather than to sell power onto the grid or to other customers. That distinguishes it from standby gensets kept purely for backup during grid outages, and from an arrangement where a third party owns the generation and sells the output to the factory under a supply agreement, which sits closer to embedded or independent power production. Our piece on [independent power producers in Nigeria](/blog/independent-power-producers-nigeria/) covers that third-party model in more detail.

In practice the line blurs. A factory can start with backup diesel sets, extend their run hours until they are effectively the primary source, and only then formalise the arrangement as a captive plant with its own fuel contracts, maintenance regime and, where the size and connection require it, its own regulatory filing.

## Diesel generating sets

Diesel remains the default starting point for a captive power plant because the equipment is familiar, lead times are shorter than for gas-fuelled alternatives, and it needs no gas connection or dedicated fuel infrastructure beyond storage tanks. Its weaknesses are the ones every plant manager already lives with: fuel cost per kWh runs well above gas alternatives, fuel quality in the local supply chain is inconsistent, and continuous running at high load hours drives shorter service intervals and faster wear on injectors, turbochargers and cylinder liners than the same engine would see on standby duty.

Where diesel still makes sense in a captive plant is as the flexible or backup leg: covering peak demand, covering periods when gas supply is interrupted, or serving sites where a gas connection is simply not available. A proper generator sizing exercise, and a clear picture of [diesel generator cost per kWh](/blog/diesel-generator-cost-per-kwh/), both belong at the start of that calculation rather than after equipment has already been ordered.

## Gas engines: pipeline gas or CNG

Gas-fuelled reciprocating engines running on pipeline natural gas, or on compressed natural gas delivered by truck where no pipeline reaches the site, cost less to run per kWh than diesel wherever fuel is reliably available, and they burn cleaner, which reduces engine wear from combustion deposits. The trade-off is dependence on gas supply and gas quality: pipeline pressure and gas composition both vary, and a captive plant built around gas engines needs a fuel supply contract and gas conditioning that a diesel-only plant does not. Our [gas generator guide](/blog/gas-generator-guide/) sets out the pipeline, CNG and quality considerations in more depth, and covers what changes when an existing diesel fleet moves onto gas.

## Gas turbines: when scale and heat rate matter

Above roughly a few megawatts, gas turbines start to compete with banks of reciprocating engines, particularly where the site can use the exhaust heat for process steam or drying, effectively running a simple combined cycle. Gas turbines need cleaner fuel gas than reciprocating engines tolerate, are less forgiving of frequent starts and stops, and their maintenance is scheduled around running hours and starts rather than a simple calendar interval. For a factory already weighing a turbine against a diesel fleet at this scale, [gas turbine vs diesel generator](/blog/gas-turbine-vs-diesel-generator/) walks through the comparison on efficiency, fuel flexibility and maintenance philosophy.

## Hybrid captive plants: adding solar and battery storage

Solar PV with battery energy storage (BESS) is increasingly added alongside diesel or gas as a hybrid captive plant rather than as a standalone source, because Nigerian daylight hours and factory shift patterns rarely line up cleanly enough for solar alone to carry a continuous industrial load. In a hybrid arrangement, solar and the battery cover as much of the daytime base load as the array size allows, the battery smooths short interruptions and absorbs load steps that would otherwise force a generator to respond, and the diesel or gas plant remains for peak demand, overnight running and periods of low solar output.

Sizing a hybrid captive plant correctly means working from an actual load profile, not an assumed one: hourly demand over a representative week, the minimum load the generating plant must be able to carry without hunting, and how much autonomy the battery needs to give before a generator has to start. Integrating solar and storage into an existing generator system also means paralleling controls, protection and, in most cases, transfer switching between sources. That work involves live electrical connections and must be carried out by qualified personnel working to a permit to work with proper isolation, not as a retrofit done around an operating plant.

## Matching the option to your load and fuel access

The table below is a starting point, not a substitute for a load study. Every captive power plant decision still has to be checked against the site's own demand profile, available fuel, space and budget for spares and skilled labour.

| Option | Typical load range | Fuel dependency | Space and infrastructure | Best fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diesel gensets | Small to large, modular | Diesel storage and delivery only | Lowest footprint, fastest to deploy | Backup, peak shaving, sites with no gas |
| Gas reciprocating engines | Small to mid, modular | Pipeline gas or CNG delivery | Gas conditioning skid, larger footprint | Continuous running where gas is reliable |
| Gas turbine (simple cycle) | Mid to large | Pipeline gas, tighter quality spec | Larger footprint, air inlet system | High running hours, process heat use |
| Solar plus BESS hybrid | Adds to any of the above | None for the solar and battery share | Roof or ground array, battery room | Daytime base load, load smoothing |

## O&M model choices

Once the equipment choice is settled, a captive power plant still needs an operating model, and this decision affects staffing, spares holding and contract structure as much as the technology choice does.

Self-operation means the factory hires and trains its own engineers, holds its own spares and runs the plant as an internal function. It gives the most control, but the factory carries the full cost of skilled staff, tooling and critical spares even at low utilisation. A contracted operation and maintenance model keeps ownership of the plant with the factory while a specialist contractor handles day-to-day operation, scheduled maintenance and fault response under a service contract, and our guide to [generator maintenance contracts](/blog/generator-maintenance-contract/) sets out what a well-structured contract should cover. A third option, third-party owned generation, has a specialist company build, own and operate the plant on or near the factory's site and sell the output under a long-term supply agreement, which moves capital risk off the factory's balance sheet but commits it to a long-term commercial arrangement and edges toward the independent power producer model described above.

Most factories that start with self-operated diesel backup migrate toward a contracted O&M model as the plant grows, because the skills needed to run gas engines or a turbine reliably, and to keep the associated safety and permit-to-work discipline current, are harder to build and retain in-house than to buy in.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between a captive power plant and a standby generator?

A standby generator exists purely to cover grid outages and normally sits idle. A captive power plant is sized and operated to be a primary or near-primary source of supply for the facility, with its own fuel contracts, maintenance regime and running hours that go well beyond backup duty.

### Can a factory mix diesel and gas generation in one captive plant?

Yes, and many Nigerian factories do exactly that, running gas engines or a turbine as the base load source while keeping diesel sets for peak demand, gas supply interruptions, or sites where the gas connection has not yet reached every building. The two fuel systems need separate storage, conditioning and control logic, which should be designed together rather than added on later.

### Is solar with battery storage enough to run a factory without diesel or gas backup?

For most industrial loads, no. Solar output varies with the season and the weather, and a factory running multiple shifts needs power overnight and on low-sun days that solar alone cannot guarantee. Solar and battery storage work best as a share of a hybrid captive plant, reducing fuel burn on the diesel or gas source rather than replacing it entirely.

### How do I decide the size of a captive power plant?

Sizing starts from a real load profile, not a nameplate total of connected equipment, because motors, compressors and process loads rarely all run together at once. A proper study measures actual demand over a representative period, including startup and step loads, and builds in margin for future expansion before any generator, engine or turbine is selected.

### Should I run the captive power plant myself or bring in a contractor for O&M?

That depends on how much skilled maintenance capacity the factory already has and how critical uninterrupted running is to production. Many factories start by self-operating diesel backup and move to a contracted operation and maintenance model as the plant adds gas engines, a turbine, or hybrid controls that need specialist skills to run reliably.
