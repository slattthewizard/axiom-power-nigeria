# Turbine Blade Damage: Causes, Detection and Repair in Nigerian Plants

A single damaged turbine blade can take a machine out of service faster than almost any other fault on a gas or steam turbine. Turbine blade failure is rarely sudden in the way it looks on the day of the trip: most blades are damaged gradually, by erosion, corrosion, deposits or fatigue, long before a crack or a liberated fragment forces a shutdown. Understanding which mechanism is at work, and catching it early, is what separates a planned blade repair from an unplanned outage that runs into weeks.

This matters more in Nigeria than in cooler, cleaner climates. Harmattan dust, variable fuel gas quality, humid coastal air in Lagos and Port Harcourt, and long continuous run hours on many captive and IPP units all push blades toward the same failure modes faster than a typical OEM service interval assumes. A maintenance manager who can read the early signs, rather than wait for a vibration alarm or a borescope surprise, keeps more of that decision in their own hands.

This article sets out the main blade damage mechanisms on gas and steam turbines, how each one is usually detected, and how a plant should think through repair versus replacement once damage is confirmed. It does not replace the OEM manual or a proper metallurgical assessment, and any work inside the turbine casing needs a permit to work, lockout/tagout and qualified personnel.

## Why Blade Damage Deserves Early Attention

Turbine blades operate at high rotational speed, high temperature (on the hot gas path side) or high steam velocity, and under continuous cyclic stress. A blade that fails in service does not fail alone. Liberated material downstream can damage several rows of blades in the same event, and the resulting imbalance can wreck bearings and seals before the protection system trips the unit. That is the difference between a scheduled blade inspection during a planned outage and an unplanned teardown that also touches the rotor, bearings and casing.

Because a full rotor inspection is expensive in both cost and downtime, most plants manage blade risk through condition indicators rather than opening the casing on a fixed calendar alone: trending vibration, exhaust gas temperature spread on gas turbines, efficiency drift, and periodic borescope work through inspection ports without a full outage. ## Erosion

Erosion is mechanical wear of the blade surface caused by particles or water droplets striking it at speed. On the compressor end of a gas turbine, the source is usually inlet air quality: dust, sand and fine particulates that get past the inlet filtration, which is a known problem during the harmattan season across northern and much of central Nigeria (see /blog/harmattan-dust-turbine-derating/ for how that dust also affects output before it becomes blade damage). On the hot gas path side, erosion can come from unburned fuel additives, carbon particles or degraded thermal barrier coating fragments circulating downstream.

On steam turbines, erosion shows up differently: water droplet erosion on the leading edges of the last low-pressure stage blades, caused by moisture forming as expanding steam approaches saturation. It is a well documented failure mode on condensing units and is one reason blade tip profiles and erosion shields exist on those last stages.

Typical signs of erosion on a borescope or a blade pulled for inspection are a thinned, roughened leading edge, loss of the aerofoil's original profile, and in advanced cases a visibly reduced chord length. Left unmanaged, erosion changes the blade's aerodynamic and structural properties enough that it becomes more vulnerable to fatigue cracking.

## Corrosion

Corrosion damage falls into two broad classes on turbines running in Nigeria. Hot corrosion affects hot gas path blades when contaminants, commonly sulphur compounds from fuel or intake air combined with salts (a real consideration for coastal sites near Lagos, Warri and Port Harcourt), attack the protective oxide layer and coating on the blade surface at high temperature. It shows as pitting, scaling and localised loss of coating, usually concentrated where gas temperature and contaminant concentration are both highest.

Pitting and general corrosion on steam turbine blades tend to come from steam chemistry: carryover of boiler water treatment chemicals, oxygen ingress, or deposits that create localised corrosive conditions under the deposit itself. This is closely tied to boiler water and condensate chemistry control, which sits outside the turbine itself but drives what the blades experience.

Corrosion pits matter beyond the material lost. Every pit is a stress concentration point, and stress concentration is where fatigue cracks start. A blade that looks only lightly pitted on visual inspection can still be a fatigue risk if the pits sit in a high-stress region of the aerofoil.

## Foreign Object Damage

Foreign object damage, usually shortened to FOD, is impact damage from an object that should never have reached the blades: a dropped tool left in the inlet duct during an outage, a piece of failed upstream hardware, ice that has broken free from inlet screens in cold climates (less relevant in Nigeria), or debris ingested through a torn inlet filter. FOD produces sharp-edged nicks, dents or tears in the leading edge, quite different in appearance from the smooth, rounded wear pattern of erosion.

The practical risk with FOD is not the visible nick itself but the crack that starts growing from its sharp edge under normal running stress. A blade nick that would be cosmetically minor on a static component can become the origin of a fatigue crack within a relatively short number of operating hours on a rotating blade. This is why FOD findings, however small, are treated as an inspection and engineering decision rather than something to note and move on from.

## Fatigue and Creep

Fatigue cracking develops from cyclic stress over time, and turbine blades see constant cyclic loading from their own rotation, from passing wakes of upstream stationary vanes, and from any resonance close to a natural frequency of the blade. Cracks typically start at a stress riser: an erosion-thinned edge, a corrosion pit, an FOD nick, a tooling mark, or a manufacturing defect, and grow slowly until they reach a critical length.

Creep is a different mechanism affecting the hottest-running blades, mainly the first stage of a gas turbine's hot gas path. It is slow, permanent deformation under sustained high temperature and stress, seen as blade elongation or tip clearance loss over thousands of running hours rather than as a crack. Creep life is one reason OEMs specify hot gas path parts as life-limited rather than run-to-failure components, and why firing temperature excursions above design get logged and reviewed even when the unit does not trip.

## Deposits and Fouling

Deposits build up on blades from contaminants in the working fluid: unburned fuel residue, salts, and particulates that stick to the blade surface rather than eroding it. On the compressor side this is often oil mist combined with dust forming a sticky film; on the hot gas path side it can be ash or mineral deposits from fuel or intake air. Fouling reduces aerodynamic efficiency, which shows up as a gradual drop in output or a rise in heat rate long before it becomes a mechanical integrity issue, and it can also mask the early stages of corrosion or erosion underneath the deposit layer.

## How Blade Damage Is Detected

| Mechanism | Typical cause | How it is usually found |
|---|---|---|
| Erosion | Inlet dust, water droplet impact (steam) | Borescope, visible profile loss, tip/chord measurement |
| Corrosion | Fuel/air contaminants, steam chemistry, coastal salt | Borescope, pitting on visual inspection, coating loss |
| Foreign object damage | Dropped tools, ingested debris, upstream hardware failure | Borescope, sharp-edged nicks distinct from erosion wear |
| Fatigue cracking | Cyclic stress starting at a stress riser | Dye penetrant/NDT during outage, vibration trend, borescope |
| Creep | Sustained high temperature and stress | Blade elongation/tip clearance trend, life-hours tracking |
| Deposits/fouling | Contaminant build-up on blade surface | Performance drift (output, heat rate), visual on inspection |

Borescope inspection remains the main tool for catching damage without opening the casing, and it is worth scheduling on a condition and running-hours basis rather than only after a trip or an unexplained performance drop. The related post at /blog/borescope-inspection-guide/ covers what a borescope inspection can see through the available access ports and where its limits are. Vibration trending and, on gas turbines, exhaust temperature spread across the measured thermocouples are the two indirect signals most plants already have instrumented and should be reviewing routinely rather than only after an alarm; /blog/turbine-inspection-intervals/ sets out how inspection frequency is usually structured against running hours and starts.

## Repair Versus Replace

Once damage is confirmed, the decision between repair and replacement depends on where the damage sits on the blade, how deep it goes relative to the blade's design wall thickness or coating allowance, and the blade's remaining life-hours if it is a life-limited hot gas path part. Minor erosion or coating loss within OEM-permitted limits is often a candidate for recoating or blending during a scheduled outage. Cracking, deep pitting, or any damage on a life-limited part close to its rated hours is a different conversation: repair may still be technically possible through an OEM-approved process, but the remaining service life after repair has to be weighed against simply replacing the part.

This decision should always sit with the OEM's technical documentation for that specific blade design and material, supported by a proper metallurgical or NDT finding, not with a visual estimate alone. A structured overhaul, covering the blade rows alongside bearings, seals and controls, gives a more complete picture than assessing blades in isolation. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common cause of turbine blade failure in Nigerian plants?

There is no single dominant cause across all sites, but inlet air quality (dust and particulates feeding erosion) and fuel or steam contamination (feeding corrosion) are the two mechanisms plants in Nigeria most often need to manage actively, alongside FOD from housekeeping lapses during outages.

### Can a cracked turbine blade be repaired instead of replaced?

Sometimes, depending on the crack's location, depth and the blade's design and remaining life-hours, and only through an OEM-approved repair process. A life-limited hot gas path blade close to its rated hours is often better replaced than repaired, but that call needs proper engineering assessment rather than a visual estimate.

### How often should turbine blades be inspected?

Inspection frequency is normally set against running hours and number of starts rather than a fixed calendar date, using borescope access between major outages and full visual or NDT inspection during scheduled teardowns. The right interval depends on the specific machine, fuel and duty cycle, so the OEM manual for that unit governs.

### Does harmattan dust actually cause blade damage, or just lost output?

Both. Dust ingestion first shows up as compressor fouling and a measurable output and efficiency loss, which is covered separately, but sustained particulate ingestion also contributes to erosion wear on compressor blades over time if inlet filtration and washing are not managed.

### What should be checked before returning a turbine to service after blade work?

Balance, tip clearances, and any borescope or dye penetrant findings on adjacent unaffected blades should be reviewed, not only the blade that was repaired or replaced, since the event that damaged one blade can leave secondary damage on neighbouring rows that is easy to miss on a quick check.
