# Band A Tariff vs Self-Generation: What the Numbers Actually Tell a Plant Manager

A plant on a Band A feeder pays Nigeria's highest electricity tariff in exchange for a stated commitment: roughly 20 hours of grid supply a day. When the feeder does not deliver that, the sums a finance team ran when the site moved to Band A stop holding up, because the plant is paying a premium tariff for hours it still has to cover with a generator. Working out whether the band a tariff makes sense for a given site, or whether it is time to lean harder on self-generation, takes more than the figure on the last bill.

This article sets out what Band A actually obligates a distribution company (DisCo) to deliver, why the naira-per-kWh figure on one plant's bill will not match another plant's, and a method for building a genuine grid-cost-per-kWh number from your own metering so it can be set against your generator's running cost. None of the naira figures below are quoted as current rates: they move with regulatory review and by DisCo, so the method matters more than any single number.

## What Band A Actually Commits a DisCo To

Nigeria has run electricity supply under a Service-Based Tariff (SBT) framework since 1 November 2020. Under SBT, customers on each feeder are classified into a band according to the minimum daily hours of supply the DisCo commits to deliver on that feeder, and the tariff a customer pays is tied to that band. According to the [Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission's (NERC) published tariff FAQ](https://nerc.gov.ng/faq/electricity-tariffs/), the bands run as follows.

| Band | Minimum daily supply committed |
|---|---|
| Band A | 20 hours or more |
| Band B | 16 hours |
| Band C | 12 hours |
| Band D | 8 hours |
| Band E | 4 hours |

A site is classified into a band based on the feeder it sits on, not on its own metering, so two factories on different feeders in the same industrial estate can sit in different bands and pay different tariffs for genuinely different service levels. Band A is the premium tier: the DisCo is committing to the longest daily supply window, and the tariff reflects that commitment. It does not mean continuous, uninterrupted power, and it does not mean the feeder cannot trip during a national grid event; it means the DisCo has agreed to a service-level target for that feeder and is billed and regulated against it.

## Why Your Band A Rate May Not Match the Plant Down the Road

NERC's tariff methodology (the Multi-Year Tariff Order, or MYTO) sets a cost-reflective trajectory and then runs periodic minor reviews that adjust the approved rate for each DisCo and band, feeding in movements in the naira exchange rate, gas price, inflation, and the energy actually available to the system. Because each DisCo has its own generation mix, loss levels, and cost base, NERC approves a different Band A rate for different DisCos even though the hours-of-supply commitment (20 hours) is the same nationally. That is why one plant's Band A bill and another plant's Band A bill, in different cities, will not line up.

This also means any naira-per-kWh figure printed in a general article is only accurate for a specific DisCo on a specific date, and it will already have moved by the time you read it. The figure that matters for your fuel and tariff budget is the one on your own current bill or your DisCo's published schedule, not a number pulled from search results. For the internal comparison that follows, treat the rate on your bill as the input, not a fixed assumption.

## What Happens When the Feeder Falls Short

Band A customers are paying for a service commitment, and NERC has shown it will act when DisCos do not meet it. In June 2026, NERC issued Directive No. NERC/2026/002 on the Special Compensation of Band A Customers Arising from Grid Generation Constraints, covering Band A feeders that recorded less than 18 hours of average daily supply in February and March 2026, a shortfall the directive attributed to gas supply constraints and damage to gas and transmission infrastructure. Affected non-maximum-demand customers were directed to receive a credit equal to 20 per cent of the approved energy cap for their feeder for the affected month, loaded to prepaid meters as token credit or applied as a bill adjustment for postpaid accounts, while retaining their Band A classification.

That directive is useful context for two reasons. First, it confirms that the 20-hour figure is treated by the regulator as an enforceable commitment, not a marketing description. Second, it shows the compensation mechanism is a partial credit against the tariff already paid, not a guarantee of supply and not compensation for the plant's own cost of running standby generation during the shortfall. A credit on next month's bill does not replace the diesel a generator burned to keep production running. That gap between what NERC compensates and what self-generation actually costs the plant is the number a maintenance or procurement team needs to build for itself.

## Building a Genuine Grid Cost Per kWh

Before comparing anything, pull the plant's own numbers rather than a headline rate. The line items to collect from a recent bill or DisCo statement:

- The energy tariff per kWh for your band, before and after VAT.
- Any fixed or demand charges billed separately from energy (maximum demand customers usually carry these).
- Metered kWh consumed in the billing period, and how many hours of actual supply the feeder delivered against the band's commitment.
- Any credits or compensation applied, so they can be netted against the true cost rather than hidden in the total.

Divide the total naira billed (energy plus fixed charges, net of any credit) by the kWh actually metered, and you have a landed grid cost per kWh specific to your site and billing period, not a national average. Because Band A rates move with each minor review, this figure should be recalculated each time a new tariff order or bill cycle lands, not treated as fixed for the year.

## Setting It Against the Generator's Real Cost Per kWh

The comparison only works once the generator side is built with the same discipline. Fuel litres per hour at the load the set actually runs (not the nameplate figure), plus lubricant, filters, scheduled service, and the capital and depreciation on the set itself, all need to be spread over the kWh the generator actually delivers, not its rated output. The methodology and the components most operators leave out (load factor, service intervals, and the cost of running a set well under its optimum load band) are covered in detail in our guide to [diesel generator cost per kWh](/blog/diesel-generator-cost-per-kwh/), which is worth working through alongside your fuel records before you compare the two numbers.

Once both sides are built on the same basis (naira per kWh actually delivered, not per kWh nameplate), the comparison stops being about which source is cheaper in principle and becomes about which mix of grid hours and generator hours produces the lowest blended cost for your actual load profile.

## A Hypothetical Worked Comparison

Take a hypothetical factory running a 400 kW average load, on a Band A feeder that delivers close to its committed 20 hours a day, with a diesel generator covering the remaining four hours plus any unplanned outage. If the grid cost per kWh built from the plant's own bill comes out lower than the generator's built cost per kWh, as it typically does even at Band A rates, the arithmetic favours maximising grid hours and running the generator only for the genuine gap, sized and maintained to start reliably and pick up load cleanly rather than to carry the plant for long stretches. If the feeder's actual delivered hours fall well short of 20 (the scenario the June 2026 compensation directive addresses), the generator's share of total kWh rises, and the blended cost per kWh rises with it even after any compensation credit, because the credit only covers part of the tariff already paid and none of the fuel burned to cover the shortfall. Neither outcome is fixed: it depends entirely on what your feeder actually delivers, which is why the comparison has to be rebuilt from your own supply log rather than assumed from the band name.

## Where This Leaves the Reliability and Downtime Question

A lower generator-only cost per kWh does not automatically make grid-first the right call for every process. If a production line's downtime cost per hour is high, the calculation has to weigh a marginally cheaper kWh against the risk of an unplanned transition when the grid feeder trips outside its committed hours, and against the transfer equipment and change-over discipline needed to make that transition clean. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Band A mean the grid will never go off at my plant?

No. Band A is a service commitment of a minimum average of 20 hours of daily supply on that feeder, set under NERC's Service-Based Tariff framework, not a guarantee of continuous power. Feeders can and do fall short of the commitment, which is why a compensation mechanism exists for shortfalls, and a plant on Band A still needs a functioning standby generator and transfer arrangement.

### Why is my Band A tariff different from a factory in another city?

NERC approves a Band A rate per DisCo, not one national figure, because each DisCo has a different generation mix, loss level, and cost base under the Multi-Year Tariff Order methodology. The 20-hour supply commitment is the same everywhere in Band A, but the rate charged for that commitment varies by DisCo and changes at each minor tariff review.

### Can I get compensation if my Band A feeder underperforms?

NERC has ordered compensation in specific cases, such as its June 2026 directive covering Band A feeders that delivered under 18 hours a day in February and March 2026, with credits calculated against the approved energy cap for the feeder. Whether a current shortfall qualifies depends on the specific NERC order in force at the time, so check with your DisCo or NERC directly rather than assuming a standing entitlement.

### Should I move my plant off Band A to save money?

That depends on your own metered numbers, not the band label. Build a genuine cost per kWh from your bill (energy charge, fixed charges, and hours actually delivered) and compare it with your generator's built cost per kWh at the load it actually runs. A lower band may cut the tariff rate but increase the hours your generator has to cover, which can raise the blended cost once fuel and maintenance are included.

### What is the biggest mistake plants make comparing grid and generator cost?

Comparing the DisCo's tariff rate directly against the generator's fuel price per litre, without converting either side into a true cost per kWh delivered. The generator side needs load factor, maintenance, and capital spread across actual kWh output, and the grid side needs fixed charges and actual delivered hours factored in, not just the headline tariff figure on the band.
