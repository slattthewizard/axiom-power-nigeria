# Cooling Tower Working Principle: How It Works, the Main Types, and What Keeps It Performing

A cooling tower is one of the least glamorous machines on a power plant, and one of the first things operators blame when output drops on a hot afternoon. Condenser vacuum slips, the steam turbine loses a few megawatts, and someone points at the tower. Sometimes they're right, and sometimes the weather is simply working against it. Knowing the cooling tower working principle is how you tell the difference.

This guide covers how evaporative cooling removes heat, range and approach, why humid southern Nigeria limits any tower, the main types, the components, and a maintenance routine. For where the tower fits in the wider plant, see [how a thermal power plant works](/blog/thermal-power-plant-explained/).

## The Cooling Tower Working Principle: Evaporation Does the Work

A tower rejects heat to the atmosphere by letting a small part of the circulating water evaporate. Evaporating water takes a large amount of latent heat from the water that stays behind, so the remainder cools. That's the whole principle. The fans, fill and nozzles exist to make evaporation happen quickly and evenly.

Warm water from the condenser or process heat exchangers is pumped to the top of the tower and sprayed over fill, a packing that breaks it into thin films or droplets. Air moves through the fill, drawn by a fan or by buoyancy. A little water evaporates, the rest drops into the cold water basin, and the circulating pumps send it back to the plant.

A useful rule of thumb is that about 1 percent of the circulating flow evaporates for every 5 to 6 K of cooling. That loss is made up continuously, and it drives the water chemistry problems later, because evaporation leaves the dissolved salts behind.

## Range, Approach and Why Wet-Bulb Temperature Sets the Limit

Two numbers describe how a tower performs. Range is the difference between hot water in and cold water out. Approach is the difference between cold water out and the wet-bulb temperature of the incoming air.

Take a hypothetical tower receiving water at 35 degrees C and returning it at 30 degrees C, on a day when the wet-bulb temperature is 25 degrees C. The range is 5 K and the approach is 5 K. Heat load and flow set the range. The tower's quality and, more than anything, the weather set the approach.

Wet-bulb temperature is what a thermometer reads with its bulb wrapped in wet cloth in moving air, so it reflects both temperature and humidity. A tower can never cool water below the wet-bulb temperature, and in practice it can't get within a few degrees of it without becoming enormous and expensive. That's why the dry-bulb number on the weather app is the wrong one to look at.

This matters in Lagos, Port Harcourt and Warri. On a hot, still, humid day the wet-bulb sits close to the dry-bulb and the air has little capacity left to absorb moisture. Cold water temperature rises, condenser pressure rises with it, and a steam turbine gives up output. In the drier north, especially during harmattan, the wet-bulb is much lower for the same dry-bulb reading, so the same tower delivers colder water.

Logging the wet-bulb reading beside each cold water temperature is the cheapest diagnostic you can have. If the approach is well wider than design, something in the tower is wrong.

## Types of Cooling Tower: Draft, Flow and Circuit

Towers are classified three ways, and any given tower is a combination of all three.

By how air moves: natural draft towers use the buoyancy of warm, moist air in a tall hyperbolic shell. You see them at very large power stations. Mechanical draft towers use fans. An induced draft cooling tower has the fan at the top, pulling air up and out through the fill. A forced draft tower has the fan at the base, pushing air in. Induced draft is the usual industrial choice because the exhaust leaves at speed, so hot, humid air is less likely to be drawn back into the intake.

By how air meets water: in a counterflow tower, air moves up while water falls down. In a crossflow tower, air moves horizontally across the falling water. Counterflow towers have a smaller footprint. Crossflow towers are wider, with open distribution basins on top that are easy to inspect.

By whether the water touches the air: an open circuit tower exposes the process water directly to air. A closed circuit tower passes the process fluid through a coil, and a separate spray loop evaporates over the coil. The process fluid stays clean, which suits engine jacket water, but these units cost more and their approach is a little poorer.

| Type | How air moves | Typical use | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Natural draft | Buoyancy in a tall shell | Large steam power stations | Very high capital cost, no fan to maintain |
| Induced draft, counterflow | Fan at top, air up against falling water | General industrial and mid-size power | Compact, fan sits in a wet, corrosive stream |
| Induced draft, crossflow | Fan at top, air across falling water | Industrial and power duty | Larger plan area, easier to inspect |
| Forced draft | Fan at base, air pushed in | Small units, tight height limits | Recirculation risk, fan motor exposed to splash |
| Closed circuit | Fan plus coil with spray loop | Clean process fluid, jacket water | Higher cost, less capacity per footprint |

## What's Inside: Fill, Drift Eliminators, Nozzles, Fans and Basin

Cooling tower fill is where most of the heat transfer happens. Splash fill uses horizontal bars to break water into droplets. Film fill uses closely spaced corrugated sheets, usually PVC, so the water spreads into a thin film with a lot of surface area. Film fill is far more efficient but also far more easily choked by scale, silt and slime. If the water treatment is poor, it's the first thing to suffer.

Above the fill, distribution nozzles or troughs spread water evenly. Blocked or broken nozzles make dry spots where air takes the easy path and the water doesn't get cooled. Above that sit the drift eliminators, which catch fine droplets before they leave in the exhaust air. Drift carries bacteria out of the tower, so a damaged eliminator is a health issue as well as a water loss.

On a mechanical draft tower the fan, gearbox, drive shaft and motor sit in a damp, corrosive, vibrating environment. The blades are usually fibreglass, set at an adjustable pitch. The gearbox reduces motor speed to fan speed, and a long drive shaft with flexible couplings connects them across the fan deck. It's a rotating equipment assembly like any other, and it needs the same [rotating equipment care](/rotating-equipment-services-nigeria/) as a pump train: alignment, balancing, gearbox condition and coupling wear.

At the bottom, the cold water basin collects the water, holds the make-up level control, and gathers whatever settles out. Basins collect silt, windblown dust and sludge, and they're ignored until the pumps start sucking air.

## Cycles of Concentration and Blowdown

Every litre that evaporates leaves its dissolved solids behind. Left alone, the water gets saltier until scale forms on every surface. To stop that, a small flow called blowdown is bled off and replaced with fresh make-up water.

Cycles of concentration is the ratio of dissolved solids in the circulating water to those in the make-up. A tower run at 4 cycles has circulating water four times as concentrated as the make-up. Make-up equals evaporation plus blowdown plus drift, and as a rough check, blowdown equals evaporation divided by the cycles minus one. Higher cycles save water but push the water towards scaling.

A water treatment specialist should set the number from an actual analysis of your make-up water. Don't copy a figure from another site.

## Cooling Tower Maintenance: Water Treatment, Fill, Fans and Basin

Honestly, most cooling tower problems are water problems. Treatment has three jobs: control scale, corrosion and biological growth. Warm, nutrient-rich, aerated water is a near-perfect culture medium. Slime and algae foul the fill, and an untreated tower can allow Legionella to grow. Breathing in contaminated drift can cause Legionnaires' disease. Control comes from a managed biocide programme, correct blowdown, intact drift eliminators and regular sampling. Your programme should come from a qualified water treatment provider, and you should check the current requirement with the relevant regulator.

A practical routine, adjusted to the site:

- Check cold water temperature against wet-bulb every shift and log both, so a widening approach shows up early.
- Inspect fill and nozzles for scale, slime and blockage, and clean or replace before airflow is choked.
- Confirm blowdown is running at the set conductivity.
- Clean the basin and strainers, scheduled around the rains and harmattan.
- Check the gearbox oil level and condition, and sample it for analysis.
- Check fan vibration, and look for loose bolts, cracked blades and a worn drive shaft coupling.
- Check fan blade pitch. A blade set too steep overloads the motor, and one set too shallow moves too little air.

Water ingress turns gearbox oil milky and ruins bearings quickly, so breathers, seals and leaks need regular attention. Vibration trending on the gearbox and fan is a good fit for [predictive maintenance](/blog/predictive-maintenance/), because the signals are slow and the failures are expensive. A rising trend gives you weeks of warning, if someone is actually looking.

## Safety: Lockout-Tagout Before You Touch the Fan

A cooling tower fan can start by itself. A temperature controller, a remote start or a well-meaning operator can spin one up while someone is standing in the fan stack. Before anyone enters the stack, works on the drive shaft, adjusts blade pitch or opens the gearbox, the motor must be isolated and locked out under your lockout-tagout procedure, with a permit to work issued and the isolation proved. Wind can also turn an idle fan, so secure it against rotation.

Basins are also confined spaces and need their own entry permit.

A tower that has lost a few degrees of approach costs you turbine output every day without ever tripping an alarm. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the basic working principle of a cooling tower?

A cooling tower cools water by evaporating a small part of it into moving air. The evaporation draws latent heat from the water that remains, so it leaves colder than it arrived. Fill spreads the water into thin films so the air can reach as much surface as possible.

### What is the difference between range and approach?

Range is the temperature drop across the tower, hot water in minus cold water out. Approach is how close the cold water gets to the wet-bulb temperature of the air. A tower can never cool below wet-bulb, so approach is the better measure of how well the tower is performing.

### Why does humid weather reduce cooling tower performance?

Humid air is already close to saturation, so it can absorb less evaporated water, and the wet-bulb temperature rises. Because cold water can only get down to a few degrees above wet-bulb, it leaves warmer. That raises condenser pressure and cuts steam turbine output.

### What is the difference between induced draft and forced draft towers?

An induced draft tower has its fan at the top, pulling air through the fill and discharging it upwards. A forced draft tower has its fan at the base, pushing air in. Induced draft is more common on industrial sites because the exhaust leaves at speed, so there's less hot air recirculation.

### How often should a cooling tower be inspected?

Operators should check cold water temperature, basin level and fan noise every shift, and walk the tower regularly. Fill, nozzles and drift eliminators need a closer look at intervals set by your water quality, and gearbox oil should be sampled on a routine schedule. A water treatment specialist should set the sampling frequency for your site.
