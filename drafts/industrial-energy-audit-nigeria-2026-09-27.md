# Industrial Energy Audit in Nigeria: What It Covers and What To Do With the Findings

A plant that has never had a proper energy audit is usually paying for waste it cannot see. The generator runs at whatever load the operator sets out of habit, not what the shift actually needs. Compressed air leaks through a dozen fittings nobody has walked past with a listening stick. Motors sized for a production line that changed years ago keep drawing the same current they always have. An industrial energy audit is the exercise that puts numbers against all of that: where the plant's kilowatt-hours and litres of diesel actually go, and which of those losses are worth fixing first.

For a Nigerian factory, oil and gas facility, hospital, bank or telecom site running mixed grid and generator power, an energy audit carries more weight than it would on a site with stable mains supply, because self-generated power is often the most expensive input on the plant's cost sheet after raw material, and in some plants ahead of labour. A watt saved on a generator-fed circuit is a watt of diesel or gas that did not have to be bought, transported, stored and burned. This piece sets out what an energy audit in Nigeria actually covers, the recognised levels of audit, what audits commonly turn up on plants like these, and how a report becomes an action plan rather than a document that sits in a drawer.

## What an Energy Audit Actually Looks At

An energy audit is a structured look at every system in the plant that consumes fuel or electricity, including the ones nobody thinks to check. On most industrial and commercial sites in Nigeria, that means:

- Metering and sub-metering: what is actually measured today, at what interval, and whether generator, grid and process loads can be told apart at all.
- Generators and prime movers: load factor against rated output, fuel consumption per unit generated, run hours, and how closely actual operation matches the duty the set was bought for.
- Compressed air systems: leak rate, pressure set point against what the process needs, and whether air is generated at a pressure higher than any downstream user actually requires.
- Motors and drives: oversizing against the driven load, whether variable-speed control would help on pumps and fans, and condition of bearings and windings that quietly raise current draw.
- Power factor and harmonics: how far the site sits from unity, what that costs on generator loading and cable heating, and whether correction equipment already installed is working.
- Lighting and HVAC: fixture type and control (timers, sensors, manual switching left running), and whether cooling load matches occupancy and equipment heat gain rather than a fixed schedule nobody has revisited.

The output is not a single number. It is a system-by-system picture of consumption, with the biggest and easiest wins ranked ahead of the marginal ones.

## The Recognised Levels of Audit

Not every plant needs the same depth of audit, and treating a first-pass audit as if it were investment-grade wastes both time and money. ISO 50002, the international standard for energy audits, sets out three levels: a walk-through audit that gives a quantitative overview and flags which systems merit closer attention, a detailed audit that measures and analyses specific systems closely enough to support quantified recommendations, and an investment-grade audit that covers every major energy-consuming system on the site in enough detail to support a capital spending decision ([ISO 50002](https://www.iso.org/standard/60088.html), Energy audits, Requirements with guidance for use).

A plant doing its first audit, or one that has never metered generator and process loads separately, gets more value from a walk-through first. It is faster, cheaper, and tells the plant where a detailed audit would actually pay for itself. A plant weighing a specific capital project, such as replacing an oversized generator or adding variable-speed drives across a pump station, needs the investment-grade level on that system, because a lender or a finance committee will ask for numbers a walk-through cannot supply.

## What These Audits Commonly Find on Nigerian Industrial Sites

The pattern repeats across plants with mixed grid and generator supply, heat, dust and inconsistent fuel quality. Take a hypothetical 500 kVA generator sized years ago for a production line that has since changed: if the plant now runs a steady 220 kVA load, the set is spending most of its life well below the load band where a diesel engine burns fuel efficiently, and an auditor will usually recommend either resizing the generator fleet or adding a smaller set for base load so the larger machine only comes on for peak demand. Our guide to [diesel generator cost per kWh](/blog/diesel-generator-cost-per-kwh/) sets out how load factor feeds into that fuel-per-unit number.

Compressed air is another repeat finding. A plant that has never walked its air lines with an ultrasonic leak detector is commonly losing a meaningful share of compressor output to leaks at fittings, hose ends and old solenoid valves, and running the header at a pressure set for one high-demand tool rather than the average user. Power factor shows up almost as often: induction motors running lightly loaded pull a lagging power factor that raises the current a generator or transformer has to supply for the same real power delivered, and that shows up as generator overload alarms long before anyone thinks to check the power factor meter. Our piece on [power factor correction and capacitor banks](/blog/power-factor-correction-capacitor-banks/) covers what fixed and automatic capacitor banks do about that and where they are worth installing.

None of this should be read as a promise of a fixed saving. Fuel, tariffs, load patterns and equipment condition differ by site, and the only honest number is the one measured on the specific plant.

## Metering: The Part Most Plants Skip

An audit is only as good as the data behind it. A site that has one meter at the generator output and nothing downstream cannot tell whether a spike in fuel use came from a compressor, a chiller or a process line that started running longer shifts. Before committing to a detailed or investment-grade audit, it is worth adding temporary or permanent sub-meters on the two or three circuits an auditor will need to isolate: the largest motor loads, the compressed air header, and HVAC if it is a significant share of the bill. A week or two of logged data on those circuits, alongside generator load logs already collected under a maintenance contract, turns an audit from guesswork into a report with real evidence behind each recommendation.

## Reading the Findings

The table below sets out the areas an industrial energy audit typically covers, what commonly turns up, and what to check before assuming the finding applies to a specific plant.

| Area | Common finding | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Generators | Running well below the load band the engine was designed for | Load logs against manufacturer load-versus-fuel curves for the specific model |
| Compressed air | Leak losses and header pressure set higher than any user needs | Ultrasonic leak survey, pressure at point of use versus at the compressor |
| Motors and drives | Oversized motors, no speed control on variable-flow duty | Nameplate rating against measured load, suitability for VFD retrofit |
| Power factor | Lagging power factor from lightly loaded induction motors | Power factor meter reading at the panel, existing correction equipment condition |
| Lighting and HVAC | Fixed schedules that ignore occupancy and actual heat gain | Control type installed, run hours against occupancy pattern |
| Metering | No sub-metering, so losses cannot be attributed to a system | What is metered today, at what interval, and whether generator and process loads are separated |

## Turning an Audit Into a Plan

A findings list is not an action plan on its own. A useful audit report separates recommendations into three groups: no-cost or low-cost operational changes (resetting a compressor pressure set point, fixing an obvious leak, adjusting an HVAC schedule), maintenance-driven fixes that belong in the existing service contract (motor bearing replacement, generator governor tuning, insulation checks), and capital items that need a business case (resizing a generator fleet, adding VFDs, installing or expanding power factor correction). Each item should carry an owner, a rough payback category rather than a guaranteed figure, and a review date, because load patterns on Nigerian industrial sites change often enough that a plan written once and filed away goes stale within a year.

It also helps to fix a review cadence rather than treating the audit as a one-off event. A short internal check on the highest-impact systems every quarter, alongside a fuller audit every one to two years, catches drift before it becomes a full rewrite of the plan. ## Who Should Run the Audit, and When

A walk-through audit is within reach of a competent in-house maintenance team with the right instruments: a power quality analyser, an ultrasonic leak detector, and access to generator run logs. A detailed or investment-grade audit, especially one that will support a capital decision, benefits from an outside engineering team that audits plants for a living and has no stake in whether the answer points at a generator, a compressor or a control system. For a plant weighing whether to bring in outside support, a [power plant audit and diagnostic](/power-plant-audit-nigeria/) covering generators, rotating equipment and power factor together, rather than three separate visits, usually surfaces interactions a single-system review would miss, such as a power factor problem masquerading as a generator sizing problem. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does an industrial energy audit in Nigeria actually cost to run?

Cost depends on the level of audit, the number of systems and buildings covered, and how much metering already exists on site. A scoped proposal following a short site visit is the reliable way to get a figure, since a generic quote cannot account for existing instrumentation or site access constraints.

### How long does a typical energy audit take?

A walk-through audit on a single site usually takes a few days of site work plus report writing. A detailed or investment-grade audit that requires logged data over one or two production cycles can run several weeks from kickoff to final report, because the measurement period matters more than the number of auditors on site.

### Can an energy audit be done without shutting down production?

Most of the work is done with the plant running normally, since the point is to measure real operating conditions rather than an idle state. A small number of checks, such as insulation resistance testing on isolated equipment, need the machine taken out of service under lockout and tagout by qualified personnel, and a competent auditor will schedule those separately.

### Does an energy audit guarantee a specific percentage saving?

No credible audit promises a fixed saving figure upfront. What it delivers is a ranked list of findings with the measurement behind each one, so the plant can decide which items to act on based on its own capital and maintenance budget rather than a generic industry average.

### How is an energy audit different from a power plant audit or diagnostic?

A power plant audit usually centres on generation equipment condition and performance, such as a turbine or generator fleet, while an energy audit looks across the whole site, including compressed air, motors, lighting and controls. On a site that generates most of its own power, the two overlap heavily and are often scoped together.
