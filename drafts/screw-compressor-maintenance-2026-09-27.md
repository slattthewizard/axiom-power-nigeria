# Screw Compressor Maintenance: Service Intervals, Failure Signs and What Actually Drives the Cost

Every factory running pneumatic tools, packaging lines, instrument air or process air depends on a screw compressor that nobody notices until it stops. Unlike a generator or a turbine, a rotary screw compressor rarely gets its own maintenance budget line. It sits in a corner of the utility room, runs continuously, and gets attention only when the discharge temperature alarm trips or the airend starts to whine. In Nigerian plant conditions, heat, dust and inconsistent grid power shorten that runway faster than the OEM manual, written for a temperate climate, assumes.

This article sets out what actually needs servicing on a rotary screw compressor and how often, what heat and dust do to that schedule, how to read oil carryover before it damages downstream equipment, the early signs of airend failure, and what genuinely drives purchase and running cost. It does not include prices: dealer and landed prices move with the exchange rate and freight cost, and any figure quoted today can be wrong by the time an order is placed.

## How a Rotary Screw Compressor Wears

Most industrial machines in Nigeria are oil-flooded rotary screw compressors: two meshing helical rotors compress air inside a housing (the airend), with injected oil sealing the clearances between rotors and housing, lubricating the bearings, and carrying away the heat of compression. Oil-free screw compressors exist for processes that cannot tolerate any oil in the air stream, such as certain pharmaceutical, food and instrumentation applications, trading that guarantee for tighter tolerances and a different service regime.

In an oil-flooded machine, the parts that wear are predictable: rotor bearings, the shaft seal, the oil separator element, and, over a long horizon, the rotor and housing clearances themselves. Everything else in the package, cooler, filters, valves, controller, exists to protect those wear points, and a maintenance plan built around that logic holds up better than one built around a generic calendar.

## Screw Compressor Maintenance: Service Items and Typical Intervals

Manufacturer datasheets for a typical industrial screw compressor set out intervals roughly like the table below. These are typical figures from OEM literature, not a substitute for the specific machine's manual, which governs on oil type, ambient temperature derating and any warranty condition.

| Interval | Typical task |
|---|---|
| Daily | Check discharge temperature, pressure and oil level; drain the condensate trap; listen for new noise or vibration |
| Weekly | Inspect for oil or air leaks at fittings and hoses; check the air filter restriction indicator; check belts or coupling on belt-driven units |
| 500 hours or per manual | Replace or clean the intake air filter; inspect the oil separator element for restriction |
| 1,000 to 2,000 hours | Oil change (mineral oils sit at the shorter end, synthetic oils can run longer, subject to oil analysis) and oil filter change |
| 4,000 hours | Oil separator element replacement |
| 8,000 hours or roughly annually | Minor service: solenoid valves, minimum pressure valve, thermostatic valve, sensor calibration check |
| 20,000 to 24,000 hours | Airend rebuild or replacement assessment, depending on condition monitoring results |

Two things move these numbers in a Nigerian plant: ambient heat and airborne dust, covered next, and duty cycle. A compressor running near full load continuously earns its service hours faster in calendar terms than one that cycles on and off, so basing a schedule on run hours rather than the calendar avoids both over-servicing and under-servicing a hard-worked machine.

## Heat and Dust: What Shortens Compressor Life in Nigerian Plant Rooms

A screw compressor rejects a large share of its input energy as heat through the aftercooler and oil cooler. Raise the ambient temperature in the plant room and the machine has less margin before its discharge temperature trip activates, which shows up as nuisance shutdowns during the hottest hours of the afternoon. Persistent high oil temperature also oxidises the oil faster than the OEM's stated drain interval assumes, one reason a calendar-based oil change is less reliable here than an oil analysis programme.

Dust does two things. It clogs the intake air filter faster, raising the pressure drop the airend works against and pushing up power draw for the same delivered air. It also fouls the cooler fins, cutting cooling capacity at exactly the time of year, the harmattan season, when ambient temperature and dust load both peak together. Plants in Kano, Kaduna and the wider harmattan belt typically need shorter filter-change and cooler-cleaning intervals during that season than the OEM's baseline schedule, and a plant room with poor ventilation or one that recirculates its own hot exhaust air compounds both problems. The same seasonal derating logic that applies to gas turbine inlet air, covered in the piece on [harmattan dust and turbine derating](/blog/harmattan-dust-turbine-derating/), applies to a screw compressor's cooling package.

## Oil Carryover: Causes and What It Costs Downstream

Oil carryover is oil present in the compressed air stream beyond the level the OEM specifies, usually stated in parts per million. Some carryover is normal and within the machine's design tolerance. The problem is carryover that climbs above that baseline, because it means oil is reaching pneumatic tools, control valves, paint lines or instrumentation that were not designed to handle it.

The usual causes are a saturated or damaged oil separator element, an overfilled sump forcing oil past the separator, a worn or misadjusted minimum pressure valve, or a worn shaft seal. Symptoms include an oily film at points of use, oil pooling in condensate drains, a steady rise in oil top-up volume, and, on contamination-sensitive processes, product or finish defects that trace back to the air supply rather than the process itself. Persistent carryover after a fresh separator element points to a mechanical fault, worth investigating before the next scheduled service rather than at it.

## Airend Failure: The Warning Signs Before It Seizes

An airend rarely fails without notice. The signs tend to build over weeks, which is why a condition monitoring routine catches them earlier than a fixed service calendar alone:

- A slow upward trend in discharge temperature at the same load and ambient conditions, rather than a single spike
- New noise: whine, knock or grinding that was not there a month earlier
- Increasing vibration at the airend bearings, picked up by a handheld meter or a fixed sensor
- Falling free air delivery for the same input power, meaning the compressor takes longer to reach cut-out pressure
- Metal particles or unusual discolouration in the oil filter or on a magnetic drain plug
- Rising oil consumption without a matching increase in visible leaks

Any of these on their own is worth a closer look; two or more together usually means the machine is approaching the airend rebuild decision in that interval table above, not the next scheduled service. The same discipline used for turbine condition monitoring, described in the note on [predictive maintenance techniques](/blog/predictive-maintenance/), applies directly here: oil analysis, vibration trending and temperature logging catch a failing airend months before a shutdown does. Any inspection that requires opening the compressor's electrical enclosure, working on the drive motor, or breaking into a pressurised line must go to a qualified technician working under lockout and tagout with a permit to work; a stored charge, a live circuit or residual system pressure has caused injuries on far simpler machines than this one.

## Leak Audits: The Maintenance Task Most Plants Skip

Compressed air is one of the most expensive utilities a factory generates per unit delivered, because every stage of compression, cooling and drying costs electricity, and electricity is the compressor's biggest lifetime cost by a wide margin (more below). A distribution network with ageing pipework, worn quick-connect couplers, leaking solenoid valves and old hose fittings wastes a share of everything the compressor produces, and that loss shows up as higher electricity consumption, not as an obvious fault.

A leak audit needs the right instrument but is straightforward in principle:

- Survey the distribution network with an ultrasonic leak detector during a quiet period, since factory noise masks the leak signature during production
- Tag each leak with its estimated size and location rather than fixing on the spot, so repairs are prioritised by loss, not convenience
- Repair the largest leaks first and re-survey on a set interval, quarterly is a reasonable starting cadence
- Extend the survey to point-of-use equipment left running when production stops, since idle consumption is as wasteful as a leak

## What Actually Drives Screw Compressor Purchase and Running Cost

Axiom's price list for services is not published here, and this is not a quote for a machine. What is worth setting out plainly is what moves the number when a plant does go to market for a screw compressor, and why the sticker price is a small part of the real cost.

The factors that move a landed price include the free air delivery (FAD) and pressure rating needed for the actual duty, not a rounded-up guess; oil-flooded versus oil-free construction; fixed-speed versus variable speed drive (VSD), which changes running cost more than purchase cost; the dryer and filtration package specified downstream; controller sophistication; and, for every imported machine, the exchange rate on the day the order is placed and the freight route it travels. Any of these can shift a quote between suppliers, which is why comparing two quotes line by line, on FAD, pressure, drive type and package, matters more than comparing headline numbers.

The bigger point is what happens after the machine is bought. U.S. Department of Energy and ENERGY STAR data on industrial compressed air systems puts electricity at roughly three-quarters of a compressor's total lifetime cost, with purchase and installation and maintenance splitting the remaining quarter between them. The same body of data estimates that unmanaged leaks account for roughly a quarter to a third of the compressed air generated in a typical industrial plant. Put together, the machine's specification and running efficiency matter more over its working life than the number on the purchase order, and a leak audit or a load-matched drive can move the electricity bill more than any negotiation on price. Where downtime from a failed compressor stops a production line, that cost compounds the point further, in the same way covered for standby power in the piece on [plant downtime cost per hour](/blog/plant-downtime-cost-per-hour/).

For a plant running compressed air, reciprocating or centrifugal gas compressors, or both, the maintenance logic is closely related; see the note on [gas compressor maintenance](/blog/gas-compressor-maintenance/) for the process gas side of the same rotating equipment fleet.

Building a maintenance plan around run hours, oil analysis and a standing leak audit, rather than a fixed annual date, is the difference between a screw compressor that reaches its rebuild interval on schedule and one that fails without warning mid-shift. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should a screw compressor's oil be changed?

It depends on the oil type and operating conditions rather than a single fixed number. Mineral oils typically sit toward the shorter end of the manufacturer's stated range, synthetic oils can often run longer, and continuous high-load operation in a hot, dusty plant room shortens whatever interval the manual states. Oil analysis at each scheduled change is the reliable way to confirm the actual interval for a specific machine rather than assuming the datasheet figure applies unchanged.

### What causes a rotary screw compressor to run hot?

The usual causes are a fouled or dirty aftercooler and oil cooler, high ambient temperature in the plant room, low oil level, a faulty thermostatic bypass valve, or restricted airflow around the unit because of poor room ventilation or nearby stored materials blocking the intake. A rising discharge temperature trend over several weeks at similar load and ambient conditions is worth investigating before it reaches the shutdown setpoint.

### Is some oil carryover in the compressed air normal?

Yes, every oil-flooded screw compressor carries a small amount of oil past the separator, and the manufacturer specifies an acceptable level in parts per million. What matters is a carryover level climbing above that baseline, which usually points to a saturated separator element, an overfilled sump or a worn shaft seal rather than normal operation.

### Does harmattan dust really affect compressor maintenance schedules?

It does. Airborne dust during the harmattan season clogs intake filters and coolers faster than a standard OEM schedule assumes, and the same period brings higher ambient temperature that reduces cooling margin. Plants in the dust belt generally need shorter filter and cooler cleaning intervals during that season than the rest of the year.

### Why does compressed air cost so much to run if the machine itself was not that expensive?

Because the purchase price is a small fraction of what a compressor costs over its working life. Industry data on industrial compressed air systems attributes roughly three-quarters of that lifetime cost to electricity, with purchase, installation and maintenance making up the rest. A compressor that looks cheap to buy but runs inefficiently, or feeds a leaking distribution network, ends up costing far more through the electricity bill than any difference in the purchase price.
