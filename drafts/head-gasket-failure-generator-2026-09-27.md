# Head Gasket Failure on Diesel Generators: Causes, Symptoms and Repair Checks

A milky sheen in the coolant expansion tank, or a film of coolant floating on top of the engine oil on the dipstick, is usually the first sign a maintenance team notices. Both point to the same likely fault on an industrial diesel generator: a failed head gasket. On a genset running long hours under Nigerian heat and dust, a head gasket that lets combustion gas, oil or coolant cross between passages it should keep separate can take a set out of service at the worst possible moment, usually during a grid outage when the plant needed it most.

This article sets out what causes head gasket failure on genset engines, the symptoms that distinguish it from other faults, how technicians confirm it before opening the engine, and what must be checked before a new gasket goes back on. Where intervals or values are mentioned, they are typical figures from manufacturer literature, not a substitute for the OEM manual on the specific engine.

## What the head gasket actually does

The head gasket sits between the cylinder head and the engine block. It has three separate jobs at once: sealing combustion pressure inside each cylinder, keeping coolant confined to the water jacket, and keeping oil confined to the lubrication galleries and camshaft area. On a genset engine that may run continuously for hundreds of hours between shutdowns, the gasket has to hold that seal through thousands of heating and cooling cycles, cylinder pressure pulses, and the small amount of flex that happens every time the head and block expand at different rates.

When the gasket fails at one of those sealing points, gas, oil or coolant crosses into a passage it was never meant to reach. Which passages are affected determines the symptoms, and the symptoms determine what a technician checks first.

## What causes head gasket failure on a generator engine

Head gasket failure is rarely a single event. It is usually the end point of a condition that built up over hours or weeks.

Overheating is the most common root cause. Repeated or sustained high coolant temperature softens the gasket material, warps the cylinder head, and in severe cases cracks the head or liner. Overheating on a genset commonly traces back to a blocked radiator core, often dust in Nigerian dry-season conditions, a failed thermostat, a slipping or seized water pump, or a coolant level that was allowed to run low.

Incorrect head bolt torque or sequence at the last rebuild is another common cause. If bolts were not torqued to the OEM specification and sequence, clamping load across the gasket face is uneven: some areas seal well, others do not, and the weak points fail under normal running pressure. On wet-liner engines, liner protrusion out of tolerance has a similar effect. The liner has to stand a specified height above the block deck so the gasket compresses it evenly, and protrusion outside the OEM tolerance, often from a liner that was not properly seated or shimmed during a previous overhaul, leaves the gasket unable to seal that cylinder consistently.

Age and thermal cycling play a part too. Every gasket has a service life, and an engine that is started and stopped frequently, or run through repeated hot shutdowns without a cool-down period, cycles the gasket harder than one that runs steadily. Detonation or pre-ignition, from contaminated fuel, incorrect injection timing or an overloaded cylinder, can produce abnormal combustion pressure spikes that a normal gasket was not designed to hold repeatedly. Coolant quality matters on the other side of the seal: coolant that has lost its inhibitor package, or plain water used without the correct additive, corrodes gasket sealing surfaces and metal fire rings from the coolant side.

## Symptoms of a blown head gasket

The classic description is oil in coolant or coolant in oil, but the actual presentation depends on which sealing point failed. Coolant in the oil shows up as a milky or mayonnaise-like appearance on the dipstick, especially after a cold start, and points to coolant crossing into an oil gallery or the crankcase. Oil in the coolant runs the other way: an oily sheen or froth sits on top of coolant in the expansion tank or radiator, the oil pressure side pushing into a coolant passage.

Other symptoms are less specific on their own but build a stronger picture together. Persistent overheating that a full coolant top-up does not fix, with the system needing repeated refills and no visible external leak, suggests combustion gas or coolant is escaping internally. White or white-grey exhaust smoke, particularly on start-up or under load, comes from coolant drawn into the combustion chamber and burned. Rough running or a miss on one cylinder can follow if coolant or a poor seal is interfering with combustion on that bore. Bubbles appearing in the coolant expansion tank while the engine is running usually mean combustion gas is pressurising the cooling system through the failed gasket, and coolant loss with no external leak anywhere on the hoses, radiator, water pump seal or block drain rounds out the picture.

If a set is showing reduced output alongside any of these signs, it is worth reading alongside our guide to [causes of low generator output](/blog/generator-low-output-causes/), since compression loss on one or more cylinders can present similarly before overheating symptoms are obvious.

## Symptom, likely cause and confirming check

| Symptom | Likely cause | Confirming check |
|---|---|---|
| Milky oil on the dipstick | Coolant entering an oil gallery | Oil analysis for glycol/coolant contamination |
| Oily film in the coolant tank | Oil pressure crossing into a coolant passage | Coolant analysis, inspect oil cooler if fitted |
| Repeated overheating, no visible leak | Combustion gas or coolant escaping internally | Cooling system pressure test, block (combustion leak) test |
| White exhaust smoke on start-up | Coolant drawn into a cylinder | Compression test by cylinder, borescope of the affected bore |
| Bubbling in the expansion tank, engine running | Cylinder pressure entering the water jacket | Block test kit (chemical combustion-gas detection) |
| Coolant loss, no external leak found | Internal leak path through the gasket or a cracked head | Full external leak check first, then pressure test |

## Confirming the failure before opening the engine

Symptoms point to a head gasket, but a technician should confirm it before committing to the labour of pulling the head, since some of the same symptoms can come from a cracked head, a failed oil cooler, or a leaking injector sleeve. A cooling system pressure test comes first: the system is pressurised to the cap rating with the engine off and cold, then watched for pressure drop and for the source of any external leak, which also rules out a bad radiator cap or hose before an internal fault is assumed.

A combustion leak, or block, test follows if the pressure test does not explain the symptoms. A chemical test kit draws vapour from above the coolant in the radiator neck or expansion tank, and a colour change confirms combustion gas is present in the coolant, strong evidence of a head gasket or cracked head rather than a coolant-quality problem. A compression or leak-down test by cylinder narrows the fault further: a cylinder with a failed gasket at that bore typically shows lower compression, or high leak-down with air audible at the radiator neck or the dipstick tube, depending on which passage the leak follows. Sending an oil and a coolant sample for laboratory analysis confirms cross-contamination even at an early stage, before it is visible to the eye, and gives a baseline to compare after the repair. Once the head is off, a visual inspection of the old gasket usually shows a burned or washed track where the failure occurred, which tells the technician exactly which cylinder and which sealing point failed, useful for the root cause conversation as well as the fix.

This work involves a pressurised cooling system, hot surfaces and rotating machinery, so it should be carried out by qualified personnel with the engine isolated, lockout/tagout applied to the starting circuit, and a permit to work in place before any cap, plug or fastener is removed.

## What has to be checked before the head goes back on

Fitting a new gasket onto a head and block that have not been properly checked is the most common reason a repair fails again within a short run time. Before reassembly, a competent shop should confirm:

- Cylinder head flatness, checked against the OEM tolerance with a straight edge and feeler gauges, or resurfaced if it is out of tolerance
- Head and block checked for cracks, particularly around exhaust valve seats and between cylinders, using dye penetrant or magnetic particle inspection where the OEM manual calls for it
- Liner protrusion remeasured and shimmed to the specified height if liners were disturbed
- Head bolt condition and torque specification, replacing bolts that are stretch-type or reused beyond the OEM's limit, and torquing in the correct sequence and stages rather than to a single final figure
- Thermostat, water pump and radiator condition, since a repeat overheating failure is likely if the root cause of the original failure was never addressed
- Coolant type and inhibitor concentration, refilled to the OEM specification rather than topped up with plain water
- A post-repair pressure test and a supervised run-up, checking temperature stability and rechecking for the original symptoms before the set is signed back into service

Take a hypothetical 500 kVA set that overheated twice in one dry season before the head gasket let go: refitting a new gasket without finding out why it overheated in the first place, whether that was a blocked radiator core, a failing thermostat or a slipping fan belt, leaves the same failure waiting to happen again on the new gasket.

## Reducing repeat failures

A head gasket failure is often the visible end of a cooling system problem that had been building for a while. A maintenance programme that checks coolant condition, radiator core cleanliness, thermostat operation and fan belt tension at every scheduled service catches most of the conditions that lead to gasket failure before they get that far. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a generator keep running with a head gasket leak?

It can run for a period depending on which passage is affected, but it should not be left running once a leak is confirmed. Continued operation risks further overheating, a cracked head, coolant reaching the cylinder and causing hydraulic lock, or contaminated oil damaging bearings, all of which turn a gasket repair into a much larger engine rebuild.

### Is milky oil always a head gasket problem?

Not always. Coolant in the oil can also come from a cracked cylinder head, a failed oil cooler on engines fitted with one, or condensation from very short running cycles in cool weather. A combustion leak test and oil analysis help separate a head gasket fault from these other causes before work begins.

### How long does a head gasket repair take on an industrial genset?

It depends on engine size, parts availability and what the inspection finds once the head is off. A straightforward gasket replacement with no head resurfacing or liner work takes less time than a repair that uncovers a warped head, damaged liners or bolt holes needing repair. Ask for a scoped assessment rather than assuming a fixed timeframe.

### What is the difference between a head gasket leak and a cracked cylinder head?

A gasket leak is a failure of the sealing material between the head and block. A cracked head is a fault in the metal casting itself, often caused by the same overheating that damages a gasket, and it will not be fixed by a new gasket alone. Pressure testing and crack detection on the head, done while it is off the engine, distinguish between the two.

### Does harmattan dust make head gasket failure more likely?

Indirectly, yes. Dust loading on radiator cores and air filters reduces cooling efficiency and can push an engine toward the overheating that damages gaskets, particularly on sites without a disciplined radiator-cleaning schedule during the dry season. Keeping the cooling system free of dust build-up is one of the more effective ways to avoid this failure.
