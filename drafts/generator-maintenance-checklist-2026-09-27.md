# Generator Maintenance Checklist for Industrial Diesel Sets in Nigeria

Most industrial generator failures trace back to a check that was skipped, not a part that failed without warning. A plant running its set 10 to 20 hours a day, in heat and dust, on fuel of variable quality, needs a generator maintenance routine that is more disciplined than the OEM's default schedule for a set doing occasional standby duty. This checklist sets out what to look at daily, weekly, monthly, and at the 250 hour, 500 hour and annual service points, and why Nigerian operating conditions push some of those tasks closer together.

None of this replaces the operation and maintenance manual for the specific engine and alternator on site. It is a working structure to build a plant's own schedule around, and a reference for what a maintenance manager should expect a contractor's report to cover.

## Why Nigerian operating conditions change the schedule

Three things push industrial diesel sets in Lagos, Port Harcourt, Abuja, Kaduna, Warri and Kano off a standard maintenance calendar. Ambient heat raises coolant and lube oil temperatures, which shortens oil life and increases the load on the cooling system. Dust and harmattan particulate load air filters faster and get past worn seals into oil and fuel where they act as an abrasive. Fuel quality varies by station and supplier, and water or sediment in a tank does more damage over long run hours than it would to a set that only starts for a monthly test.

The result is that sets doing genuine prime or continuous duty in Nigeria often need oil, filter and coolant intervals tightened from what the OEM manual lists for a temperate climate and light duty cycle, and need daily checks that a lightly used standby set could skip. A set run past its rated duty class, or run hard on poor fuel, will show it first in oil analysis and coolant condition long before a fault code appears.

## Daily generator checks

These take a few minutes before start and a few minutes after shutdown, and they catch most of the faults that would otherwise turn into a call for emergency repair.

- Engine oil level and visual condition (colour, smell of fuel, milkiness suggesting coolant ingress)
- Coolant level in the header tank or radiator, and any sign of leaks at hoses, the radiator core or the water pump
- Fuel level and a look for water or sediment at the tank drain
- Battery terminals, charge state and the battery charger's output
- Air filter restriction indicator, and a walk-round for oil, fuel or coolant leaks under the set
- Exhaust colour and unusual noise or vibration once running
- Control panel alarms and any events logged since the last check

## Weekly and monthly checks

Weekly checks add depth to the daily walk-round: belt tension and condition, radiator core cleanliness (dust builds fast in harmattan months), fuel filter and water separator condition, and a check of the automatic transfer switch or changeover arrangement if the set backs one up. Load bank testing on a schedule, as covered in our guide to [generator load bank testing](/blog/generator-load-bank-testing/), catches wet stacking and low output before they show up as a failed transfer during a mains outage.

Monthly checks move into instrumentation and control: insulation resistance trending on the alternator windings, governor and AVR response under a test load step, coolant additive or inhibitor level, and a full download and review of the controller's event log rather than just the current alarm screen. A monthly review is also the point to reconcile actual run hours against the plan, since a set running more hours than expected will hit its 250 hour and 500 hour service points sooner than the calendar suggests.

## Generator maintenance intervals: 250 hour, 500 hour and annual service

The 250 hour point is the one most manufacturer service literature centres on for oil and primary filter changes on a working diesel set. [Cummins service literature on diesel generator set reliability](https://mart.cummins.com/imagelibrary/data/assetfiles/0056662.pdf) lists oil, filter and belt tension checks around the 250 to 300 hour mark for sets in regular duty, with valve clearance checks and a fuel system inspection at longer intervals measured in thousands of hours, and stresses that the interval for a specific model is set by its own operation and maintenance manual, not by a generic table. Treat any interval quoted here as typical, not as a substitute for that manual.

For a standby set that rarely runs enough hours to reach 250 hours in a year, the calendar takes over: oil and coolant are serviced on a fixed annual or semi-annual date regardless of hours, because oil degrades with age and moisture ingress even when the engine barely turns. A set doing genuine prime duty in Nigeria, running most days, will usually hit its hour-based service points first.

The 500 hour point typically repeats the 250 hour tasks and adds a closer look at the fuel injection system, turbocharger condition, and alternator bearing grease or condition depending on design. Annual service, whichever comes first by hours or by date, is the point for coolant replacement, a full load test, thermal imaging of electrical connections, and a review of everything the daily and weekly checks logged over the year.

| Interval | Typical tasks | Why it matters in Nigerian conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Daily | Fluid levels, leak check, alarm log, visual walk-round | Catches low oil, coolant loss and fuel water before a run |
| Weekly | Belts, radiator core, fuel filters, load test where scheduled | Dust loads the radiator and filters faster than temperate climates |
| Monthly | Insulation resistance trend, AVR/governor response, event log review | Trends catch drift before a fault trip |
| 250 hours | Oil, primary filters, belt tension, general inspection | Heat and dust shorten oil life versus the OEM base case |
| 500 hours | Repeat 250 hour tasks, fuel system, turbocharger, alternator check | Longer run hours bring fuel and turbo wear into range |
| Annual | Coolant change, full load test, thermal imaging, year-end review | Age-related degradation on lightly run standby sets |

## What skipping the checklist actually costs

A generator that runs light or idles for long periods without reaching full load, a common pattern on oversized or lightly loaded standby sets, is at risk of wet stacking: unburned fuel and carbon accumulating in the exhaust and cylinders because combustion temperatures never get high enough to burn it off cleanly. Our article on [diesel generator wet stacking](/blog/diesel-generator-wet-stacking/) sets out the mechanism and the load bank testing that prevents it. A checklist that includes a genuine load step rather than only a visual inspection is what catches this before it becomes an engine problem.

Skipped fuel checks are the other common route to an expensive failure. Water and sediment in stored diesel get past a worn filter and reach the injection pump and injectors, where contamination wears precision components that are slow and costly to replace. A daily tank drain check and a fuel quality check on delivery cost minutes; an injection pump rebuild does not.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should an industrial generator be serviced?

It depends on run hours and duty class rather than a single fixed answer. A set doing genuine prime duty commonly reaches its 250 hour service point every few weeks, while a lightly used standby set may only reach it once or twice a year and gets serviced on a calendar basis instead. The OEM manual for the specific engine sets the governing figure.

### What is the difference between a 250 hour and 500 hour generator service?

The 250 hour service is mainly oil, primary filters and a general inspection. The 500 hour point usually repeats those tasks and adds checks further into the fuel system, turbocharger and alternator that are not needed as often. Both intervals shift earlier if the set operates in heavy dust or high ambient heat.

### Can daily generator checks be done by non-technical staff?

Visual checks such as fluid levels, leaks, alarm screens and unusual noise can be assigned to trained site staff with a simple form. Anything that involves opening electrical panels, working near rotating parts, or testing under load needs a qualified technician working to a permit to work with proper isolation, not an untrained staff member.

### Does harmattan dust change the generator maintenance schedule?

Yes. Airborne dust loads air filters and radiator cores faster during harmattan months, which is why weekly rather than monthly checks on those items make sense for that period. Restriction indicators should be watched more closely, and filter change intervals often need to be shortened for the season rather than left at the standard figure.

### What should a generator maintenance report include?

A useful report lists the interval covered, every task carried out with a pass or fail against a measured value rather than a tick, any part replaced with its running hours at replacement, oil and coolant condition readings where taken, and open items still needing attention with a target date. A report that only says "serviced, no issues" is not enough to plan around.
