# Transformer Oil Testing: BDV, Moisture, DGA and What the Results Actually Mean

A transformer can't be inspected like a motor. You can't open it up, look at the winding and listen for a bearing. The oil is the only part of the machine that's both inside it and available to you, which is why transformer oil testing is the closest thing a plant has to a medical check-up for its transformers. The oil carries heat out of the windings, insulates the live parts, and, as it ages and as faults develop, it collects evidence of what's going on in there.

The problem on most sites isn't that nobody tests. It's that someone takes a sample, a lab sends back a table of numbers, and the report goes into a folder. Nobody on the plant knows which number matters, or whether a bad sample made a healthy transformer look sick. This article goes through each common transformer oil test, what it tells you, how to take a sample that won't mislead you, and when the right answer is to filter the oil rather than replace it.

## What transformer oil does, and why it's worth testing

Mineral insulating oil does two jobs at once. It's a dielectric, so it has to hold off the voltage between windings, core and tank. And it's a coolant, carrying heat from the windings to the radiators or tank walls. Oil in service is slowly attacked by heat, oxygen and moisture, and the cellulose paper wrapped around the conductors is attacked with it. Once paper has aged, it doesn't recover. Oil can often be restored, but the paper's life is spent for good.

That's the reason oil testing gets treated as a condition-monitoring tool and not just a quality check. A few tests describe the oil itself (breakdown voltage, moisture, acidity, interfacial tension, colour). One family, dissolved gas analysis, describes what the equipment is doing. And one, furan analysis, is a proxy for the condition of the paper. Read together they give you a trend, and the trend is worth far more than any single result. If you already run a [predictive maintenance](/blog/predictive-maintenance/) programme, oil data is among the cheapest inputs you can feed it.

## The routine tests and what each one tells you

Here's the short version, then the detail.

| Test | What it measures | What a bad result points to |
|---|---|---|
| Breakdown voltage (BDV) | Voltage at which the oil sample fails electrically | Water, dissolved or free, plus particles, fibres, dirt |
| Moisture content (Karl Fischer, ppm) | Water dissolved in the oil | Seal leaks, breather problems, paper ageing, poor handling |
| Acidity (neutralisation number) | Acid by-products of oxidation | Oil ageing, overheating, sludge risk |
| Interfacial tension (IFT) | Surface tension between oil and water | Polar contaminants, early oxidation, sludge precursors |
| Colour and appearance | Visual darkening, cloudiness, particles | Oxidation, contamination, free water |
| Dissolved gas analysis (DGA) | Gases generated by faults, dissolved in the oil | Overheating, partial discharge, arcing |
| Furan analysis | Chemical by-products of paper breakdown | Advanced ageing of the paper insulation |

### Breakdown voltage (the BDV test)

The BDV test puts a sample of oil between two electrodes at a set gap and raises the voltage until the oil breaks down and a spark jumps. The voltage at which that happens is the result. It's quick, cheap and can be done in a basic oil lab or with a portable tester, which is why the BDV test is the one most Nigerian plants have heard of.

What it's good at is catching water and particles. A drop of moisture or a few conducting fibres in the gap makes the oil fail early. What it's bad at is telling you why. A low BDV tells you the oil is no longer a good insulator, not whether the cause is water, dirt, or a poorly cleaned test cell. Treat it as a screening test, and be suspicious of a single odd value. Repeat samples, and look at moisture alongside it.

### Moisture content

Moisture is measured by Karl Fischer titration and reported in parts per million (ppm) by weight of oil. Water is the enemy of both oil and paper. It lowers the dielectric strength of the oil, it speeds up paper ageing, and at high enough levels it can come out of solution and sit as free water.

Here's the catch most people miss. Most of the water in a transformer is in the paper, not the oil, and the way it splits between the two depends on temperature. Warm oil holds more water than cold oil. So the same transformer can show different ppm at different temperatures, and a sample taken from a hot transformer under load reads differently from one taken at ambient on a cold morning. Good labs and good reports record the oil temperature at sampling so the moisture can be interpreted properly. If your report doesn't carry that temperature, ask why.

### Acidity (neutralisation number)

Oxidising oil forms organic acids. The neutralisation number measures how much of a reagent it takes to neutralise them, and it climbs as oil ages. Rising acidity attacks paper and metal and feeds sludge formation. It's a slow-moving number, so what matters is the trend over several samples, not a single reading. A sudden step up in a short period suggests something is wrong with the cooling, the load or the oil's protection from air.

### Interfacial tension

IFT measures the tension at the boundary between oil and water. Clean new oil holds water at arm's length, so the tension is high. As oxidation products and other polar contaminants build up, the tension drops. It's an early indicator, often moving before acidity does and before sludge forms. A falling IFT alongside rising acidity is a consistent story of an oil that's ageing and may be worth reconditioning or replacing.

### Colour and appearance

It sounds crude, but the colour test earns its place. Fresh oil is pale. Darkening over time points to oxidation or contamination, and a sudden change between two samples is worth chasing. Cloudiness suggests water. Visible particles or fibres suggest paper, dirt or metal. Look at the sample in the bottle before it goes anywhere. A technician who writes "cloudy, free water visible" on the sampling label has saved the lab a day.

## Dissolved gas analysis: what the transformer is telling you

DGA is the most valuable single test on the list. When oil or paper is stressed by heat, partial discharge or arcing, they break down and produce gases. Those gases dissolve in the oil. A lab extracts them and measures what's there. Different faults produce different gas mixes, which is what makes the test diagnostic and not just descriptive.

The key gases and the fault types they usually point to are:

- Hydrogen, produced in almost every fault type, and the main gas from partial discharge (corona) in the oil. A rising hydrogen level on its own is a prompt to look closer, not a verdict.
- Methane and ethane, which rise with low to moderate thermal faults in the oil.
- Ethylene, which becomes dominant as the temperature of a thermal fault rises. High ethylene points to a hot spot, often a poor connection, a circulating current or a core problem.
- Acetylene, the one that gets people's attention. It's formed at very high temperatures and is the signature gas of arcing. Any acetylene appearing in a transformer that had none before deserves immediate attention.
- Carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide, which come from the paper. Rising CO and CO2 point to overheating of or involving cellulose insulation, and the ratio between the two is used as a guide to paper involvement.

Interpretation of these gases is codified in IEC 60599, which sets out how to read gas concentrations, gas ratios and rates of change. Laboratories and engineers work from it, and so should you if you're reviewing results. We're not going to quote its numbers here. The thresholds depend on the transformer type, age and sampling history, and a number lifted out of context is the surest way to misdiagnose a unit.

What matters in practice is the rate of gas generation, not just the absolute level. A transformer that's stable at a modest gas level for years is a very different case from one whose hydrogen and ethylene doubled between two samples. That's another reason to keep the full history and not just the latest report.

## Furan analysis: reading the paper

Oil tests describe the oil. They can't directly tell you how much life is left in the paper, and the paper is what ultimately limits a transformer's life. When cellulose breaks down it releases furanic compounds, which dissolve in the oil. Measuring them gives an indirect estimate of paper ageing without taking the transformer apart.

Furan analysis isn't a routine annual test on every unit. It's most useful on older, larger or more critical transformers, when DGA shows CO and CO2 trending up, or when you're deciding whether a life extension is worth the money. Be aware that it has its own limits. Oil changes, reconditioning and some oil treatments alter the furan content, so a result needs to be read against the unit's maintenance history. Quote it as an estimate, never as a precise remaining-life number.

## Sampling: where good data goes bad

You can lose all the value of a test at the sampling stage. The lab can only report what's in the bottle, and a bad bottle gives a confident wrong answer. The common pitfalls are:

- Moisture getting into the sample. Taking a sample on a humid day or in rain, leaving the bottle open, or letting the sample sit before it's sealed can raise the measured water. Sample in dry conditions where you can, and keep the sample sealed as soon as it's drawn.
- Dirty or unsuitable containers. Use clean, dry bottles or syringes supplied for the purpose and don't reuse a bottle that held something else. Residues will ruin the BDV and moisture readings and can disturb the gas results.
- Not flushing the valve. The oil sitting in the sampling valve and pipe is stagnant and unrepresentative. Flush a suitable volume through first and dispose of it properly before filling the sample container.
- Sampling from the wrong place. The usual point is the bottom drain valve, because it's where water and sediment collect. That's useful for catching contamination, but remember it's a worst-case location, so an elevated result there is a signal to investigate, not proof that the whole tank is bad. Use the sampling point the unit was built with and note which one you used each time, so the results stay comparable.
- Ignoring temperature. Record the oil temperature at the time of sampling, and the load if you know it. Moisture results in particular can't be read without it.
- Gas loss in DGA samples. Gas dissolved in oil can escape through an air gap or a poor seal. DGA samples are normally drawn into gas-tight syringes or sealed vessels, so follow the lab's instructions to the letter.

Safety matters here. Sampling from an energised transformer is hands-on work near live equipment. Follow your site's permit to work system, apply isolation and lockout-tagout where the sampling point or the circuit requires it, and have the work carried out by someone competent to do it. Hot oil, pressure in the tank and live conductors are all real hazards. Don't open a valve on a transformer unless you've confirmed it's safe to do so under the permit.

## How often should you test?

Treat this as general guidance only. The right interval depends on the transformer's size, criticality, age, loading, environment and what the last results showed. As a rough pattern, a lot of operators run a basic set of oil tests on a regular cycle, often annually for distribution-class units and more often for larger or critical ones. They add DGA on a similar or more frequent schedule for power transformers, shorten the interval when gas levels rise, and run furan analysis less often, mainly on ageing or critical units. Any unit that has just tripped on protection, been through a through-fault, or shown an alarm needs a sample soon after the event, not at the next scheduled date.

IEC 60422 is the standard that covers supervision and maintenance of mineral insulating oils in service, including the kind of monitoring a plant should have in place. Use it as the reference when you set up your own schedule and check the current edition, since interval and limit guidance in it is a matter for your transformer's duty. ## Filtering, reconditioning or replacing the oil

When results come back poor, the instinct is to change the oil. Often that's the wrong first move, and sometimes it's the right one for the wrong reason.

Where the problem is water and particles, and the oil chemistry is otherwise acceptable, filtration or vacuum dehydration can restore it. A filter-and-degas pass pulls out moisture, dissolved gas and solids, and the BDV result recovers. Honestly, a lot of oil that gets scrapped could have been cleaned for less money and less waste.

Where acidity is high and IFT has dropped, the oil is chemically aged, and simple filtering won't fix that. Reconditioning with an adsorbent clay treatment can restore some properties in oil that hasn't gone too far. If the oil is badly degraded, or sludge has already formed, replacement can be the better choice, usually after flushing the windings and tank.

Two cautions. First, cleaning oil doesn't clean paper. Moisture locked in the cellulose will migrate back into fresh oil over time, so a dried-out oil result can creep up again within months if the paper is still wet. Second, don't confuse treating the oil with fixing the cause. If water keeps getting in, the answer is a bad gasket, an exhausted silica gel breather or a leaking seal, and that has to be found and repaired. And if DGA points to an active fault, no amount of filtering will help. It needs a proper inspection, and if you've found the oil isn't the only concern, a wider [power plant audit](/power-plant-audit-nigeria/) can show where the rest of the electrical system stands.

Oil work on a transformer is hands-on and needs a permit to work, isolation and lockout-tagout, and competent people. Don't treat it as a routine topping-up job.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a BDV test on transformer oil?

A BDV (breakdown voltage) test applies a rising voltage across a fixed gap in a sample of oil until it breaks down electrically. The voltage reached tells you how well the oil can insulate. A low value usually points to water or particles in the oil, so it's best read together with a moisture test.

### What is dissolved gas analysis in a transformer?

Dissolved gas analysis, or DGA, measures the gases that overheating, partial discharge and arcing release into the oil. Hydrogen, methane, ethane, ethylene, acetylene, carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide each point towards particular fault types. The trend between samples matters more than any one result.

### How often should transformer oil be tested?

It depends on the unit's size, age, criticality and loading, so treat any fixed interval as general guidance. Many operators test routinely once a year and test more often when results change. An unexplained alarm, trip or through-fault should prompt a sample straight away.

### Can contaminated transformer oil be cleaned instead of replaced?

Often yes. Water, dissolved gas and particles can be removed by filtration and vacuum dehydration, and mildly aged oil can sometimes be reconditioned. Badly oxidised oil with high acidity and sludge is usually better replaced. Cleaning the oil won't dry out wet paper, and it won't repair a fault that DGA has flagged.
