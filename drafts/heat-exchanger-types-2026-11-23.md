# Types of Heat Exchangers in Power and Process Plants: How They Work and How They Fail

Walk round any power plant and you'll find heat exchangers on almost every skid. The lube oil cooler on a turbine, the jacket water cooler on a diesel set, the intercooler on a compressor, the condenser under a steam turbine. They have no moving parts, so they get ignored until a temperature alarm goes off or a trip takes the machine down.

This guide covers the main types of heat exchangers on industrial plant, how they work and how to keep them healthy.

## The working principle in plain words

Every heat exchanger does one job. Heat moves from a hotter fluid to a cooler one through a wall, and the two fluids never mix. How much heat moves depends on three things: the area of the wall, how good the wall and the films of fluid on each side are at passing heat, and the temperature difference driving it.

That last point is where flow arrangement matters. In parallel flow, both fluids enter at the same end and travel the same way. The temperature gap is large at the inlet and collapses towards the outlet, so the cold fluid can never get hotter than the hot fluid leaving. In counterflow, the fluids run in opposite directions. The gap stays fairly even along the whole length, and the cold fluid can leave hotter than the hot fluid does at its own outlet. For the same area, counterflow moves more heat. That's why most serious duties are designed counterflow, or as close to it as the geometry allows.

Designers use the log mean temperature difference, usually shortened to LMTD, to do the sums. In words, it's an average of the temperature gap at the two ends of the exchanger, weighted so that the end with the smaller gap counts for more. You won't calculate it on site. The practical point is the approach temperature, the gap between the hot outlet and the cold inlet: when it widens at the same load, the exchanger is losing capacity.

## Shell-and-tube exchangers

The shell and tube heat exchanger is the workhorse of heavy industry. A bundle of tubes sits inside a cylindrical shell. One fluid runs through the tubes, the other around them, guided by baffles that force it to cross the bundle instead of sliding straight past. The tube side is normally the dirtier or higher-pressure fluid because tubes are easier to clean and easier to plug.

There are three common constructions, and the difference matters when you're planning a shutdown.

Fixed tubesheet units have the tubes welded or expanded into a plate at each end, and both plates are welded to the shell. They're cheap and tight, but the shell side can't be pulled out for cleaning, and a large temperature difference between shell and tubes puts the tubes under stress unless there's an expansion joint.

U-tube units bend each tube into a hairpin, so only one tubesheet is needed. The bundle can be withdrawn and the shell side cleaned. The weak spot is the bend itself, and the inner tubes of the bundle are awkward to clean mechanically.

Floating head units let one tubesheet move freely with thermal growth. The bundle pulls out completely, so both sides can be cleaned. On dirty services they're the sensible choice, despite the extra joints to leak. Honestly, if you can't tell which type you've got, check the drawing before you start a pull.

## Plate exchangers

A plate heat exchanger is a stack of thin corrugated metal plates clamped between two end covers. The fluids pass through alternate channels, and the corrugations create turbulence that makes them very efficient. You get far more heat transfer for the footprint than a shell-and-tube unit.

Gasketed plate units use a rubber or synthetic gasket around each plate. Undo the clamping bolts and the pack opens like a book, so cleaning and inspection are easy, and you can add plates if the duty grows. The catch is the gaskets, which age and harden.

Brazed plate units have the plates bonded permanently into a sealed block. They're compact and cheap for small duties, such as a lube oil or hydraulic cooler on a skid, but they can't be opened. When one fouls beyond what a chemical flush can fix, you replace it.

Narrow channels block easily, so fit a strainer upstream.

## Air-cooled and double-pipe exchangers

Air-cooled exchangers, often called fin-fan coolers, pass the process fluid through finned tubes while fans push or pull ambient air across them. They're what you use where cooling water is scarce or poor, which is common on remote plant. The price is size, fan power and sensitivity to ambient conditions. In harmattan dust the fins load up and outlet temperatures creep up. Washing the fins is the cheapest maintenance on the plant.

Double-pipe exchangers are the simplest form, one pipe inside another. They suit small, high-pressure duties such as sample coolers, and scale poorly.

## Types at a glance

| Type | Best at | Weak point | Typical cleaning route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed tubesheet shell-and-tube | Clean shell-side service, low cost | Shell side can't be pulled | Tube side mechanical or chemical |
| U-tube shell-and-tube | High temperature or pressure differences | Tube bends are hard to clean | Chemical, then hydro-jetting where reachable |
| Floating head shell-and-tube | Dirty fluids on both sides | More joints, higher cost | Pull the bundle, jet both sides |
| Gasketed plate | High efficiency, easy to open | Gasket ageing, particles | Open the pack, clean plates by hand |
| Brazed plate | Compact small duties | Can't be opened | Chemical flush or replace |
| Air-cooled fin-fan | No cooling water needed | Fin fouling, fan power | Wash fins, check fan drive |
| Double pipe | Small, high-pressure duties | Poor scale-up | Mechanical, by opening the hairpin |

## Where they sit on power and process plants

On a turbine, the lube oil cooler is usually a shell-and-tube or plate unit, and its job is to hold bearing supply oil in a narrow temperature band. If it fouls, oil runs hot, viscosity falls and the film in the bearings thins. Oil analysis shows the result afterwards, as covered in our guide to [turbine lube oil analysis](/blog/turbine-lube-oil-analysis/).

On a diesel or gas engine, you'll find the jacket water cooler and the charge air cooler. A fouled charge air cooler means hotter air into the cylinders, higher exhaust temperatures and lost power. A fouled jacket water cooler pushes the engine towards high-temperature trips.

Steam plant adds the surface condenser, a large shell-and-tube exchanger that turns exhaust steam back to water under vacuum. If you want the bigger picture of how it fits in, see [how a thermal power plant works](/blog/thermal-power-plant-explained/).

Compressors carry intercoolers and aftercoolers, which cool gas between stages and before it goes downstream. Our notes on [gas compressor maintenance](/blog/gas-compressor-maintenance/) and [screw compressor maintenance](/blog/screw-compressor-maintenance/) cover the cooler checks that belong in a routine.

## Fouling and how to clean it

Heat exchanger fouling is any deposit on the heat transfer surface that adds resistance. Scale from hard water, silt and sand, biological slime, corrosion products and oil carry-over are the usual culprits. Even a thin layer insulates, and it narrows the passage, so pressure drop rises as heat transfer falls.

Which method you use depends on what the deposit is and how you can reach it.

Chemical cleaning circulates an acid or other solvent through the exchanger to dissolve scale and rust. It reaches places a lance can't, such as U-bends and brazed plate channels. Choose the chemical against the material list, and neutralise and flush afterwards.

Mechanical cleaning uses rods, brushes and drills pushed through the tubes. It works on hard deposits in straight tubes, but a scratched wall fouls faster next time.

Hydro-jetting fires high-pressure water through a lance down each tube, or across a pulled bundle. It's quick and effective on soft and moderately hard deposits. The operator needs training and proper protection, because the pressures are dangerous.

Before any of this, the exchanger must be isolated, drained, depressurised and cooled, and the work must run under a permit to work with lockout-tagout on every connected valve and pump.

## Leaks, gaskets and finding the fault

A leaking tube lets the high-pressure side contaminate the low-pressure side. On a lube oil cooler, that might be water in the oil or oil in the cooling water. On a jacket water cooler, it might be coolant loss or combustion gas. Early signs are slow top-up demand, milky oil or unexplained chemical dosing.

To find the leak, shut down and isolate under a permit to work. Then drain one side and pressurise the other with water, or air for a gas test, watching the empty side for drips or a falling pressure gauge. A hydrostatic test is the usual method for tubes. For tight or hard-to-see leaks, a helium test or tracer gas gives a clearer answer, because the gas escapes through tiny defects and a sniffer shows exactly where. Plugging the leaking tube is the standard repair, but each plug cuts capacity, so count them.

On gasketed plate units, a leak is often a gasket, not a plate. Replacement means releasing the pack under controlled conditions, cleaning the gasket grooves, fitting new gaskets in the right elastomer, and tightening the pack back to the manufacturer's stated dimension.

## Monitoring performance before it fails

You don't need special instruments to catch a degrading exchanger. You need trend data you already have, read properly.

Approach temperature is the first number to watch. For a given load and cooling medium, the gap between the hot outlet and the cold inlet should be steady. If it widens over weeks, the exchanger is losing capacity. Pressure drop across each side is the second. A rising pressure drop with the same flow points to blockage or fouling. A falling one with falling flow can mean a pump or valve issue, not a heat exchanger one.

Log both against load and ambient temperature, so you aren't fooled by a hot day or a half-loaded machine. Clean on trend, not on a calendar. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the main types of heat exchangers used in industry?

The common ones are shell-and-tube, gasketed and brazed plate, air-cooled fin-fan, and double-pipe units. Shell-and-tube is the usual choice for high pressures, large duties and dirty fluids. Plate exchangers win on compact size and efficiency where the fluids are clean.

### What is the difference between counterflow and parallel flow?

In parallel flow both fluids move in the same direction, and in counterflow they move in opposite directions. Counterflow keeps a more even temperature gap along the length, so it moves more heat for the same surface area.

### How do I know a heat exchanger is fouled?

Look at the trend in approach temperature and pressure drop. If the outlet temperature drifts up at the same load and cooling conditions, and the pressure drop across the exchanger rises, deposits are the likely cause.

### Can heat exchanger tubes be repaired?

Often, yes. A leaking tube can be plugged at both ends, which isolates it from service. That reduces the available surface area, so if plugging passes a limit set by the duty margin, the bundle should be retubed or replaced. Brazed plate units are replaced whole.

### Is it safe to open a heat exchanger while the plant is running?

No. The unit must be isolated, drained, depressurised, cooled and locked out under a permit to work before anyone removes a cover or a head. A closed valve alone isn't isolation.
