# Independent Power Producers in Nigeria: What Plant Managers Need to Know

A plant manager weighing options for a factory or facility with unreliable grid supply usually hits the same wall: build a captive plant and carry all the operating risk, or buy from a third party and carry counterparty risk instead. Independent power producers in Nigeria are that third-party option, and the terms "IPP", "embedded generator" and "captive power" get used loosely in commercial conversations even though they mean different things under the regulatory framework. Getting the distinction right matters before a procurement officer signs anything, because it changes who holds the generation licence, who is liable for an outage and how a facility's own maintenance obligations are structured.

This article sets out what an independent power producer and an embedded generator are under Nigerian regulation, how industrial clusters typically buy power from an IPP, and what operation and maintenance obligations and reliability actually look like once the contract is signed. It is written for the buyer's side: the plant, maintenance or procurement manager evaluating a supply arrangement, not the IPP itself.

## What is an independent power producer in Nigeria?

Under the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission's (NERC) generation licensing framework, a generation licence can be held by a successor company from the old PHCN structure or by an independent power producer, meaning any other company that builds, owns and operates a generation asset under its own licence. NERC issues generation licences in three categories, described on its own [licensing page](https://nerc.gov.ng/need-help/services/generation-licence/) as on-grid (connected to the national transmission network), off-grid (standalone, not connected to the grid) and embedded (connected to the distribution network). An IPP can sit in any of these three categories depending on where its plant connects and who it sells to.

The practical distinction a plant manager needs is not "is this company an IPP" (most third-party generators are), but which licence category the specific project holds, because that dictates whether the plant buys power through a distribution company's network, through a direct line, or off the national grid.

## Embedded generation: the category that matters most to industrial clusters

Embedded generation is defined as generation that connects and evacuates its output through a distribution system, or through an independent distribution licensee, rather than through the national transmission network. In practice this means the generator's plant sits close to the load, often inside or adjacent to an industrial estate, and supplies power through the local distribution company's wires or through a private distribution arrangement covering that estate.

Embedded generation was designed to let an IPP add capacity into a constrained network without the cost and lead time of a transmission connection, and it is the category most industrial clusters actually deal with when a "power company" offers to supply a factory or an estate. An embedded generator selling into a cluster still needs a generation licence and, where it also owns the wires between the plant and the buyers, may need a separate distribution arrangement recognised by NERC.

## How industrial clusters buy power from an IPP

An industrial park or a group of factories sharing one feeder typically buys from an embedded IPP through one of a small number of structures:

- A bulk supply or power purchase agreement between the IPP and an estate management company, which then bills individual tenants.
- Direct bilateral power purchase agreements between the IPP and each large offtaker on the cluster, metered separately.
- A hybrid where the IPP supplies base load and each factory keeps its own standby generator for the balance, sized against the IPP's contracted capacity rather than full plant demand (see our [generator sizing guide](/blog/generator-sizing-guide/)).

Whichever structure is used, the commercial terms a buyer should pin down before signing include the contracted capacity and how it is metered, the tariff basis and how it moves with fuel cost and the exchange rate, the availability guarantee (if any) and how shortfall is compensated, and the notice period for planned outages. None of these figures are standard across the market: they are negotiated per project and per fuel type, and a buyer should treat any tariff quoted verbally as indicative until it is in a signed schedule.

## O&M obligations: whose job is what

A generation licence puts the obligation to operate and maintain the plant safely and to the standard NERC expects on the licensee, which is the IPP, not the offtaker. That obligation typically covers routine and major maintenance of the generating units, compliance with technical and safety codes, metering accuracy, and reporting of outages. What it does not automatically cover is the offtaker's own switchgear, transformers and internal distribution from the point of supply into the factory: that boundary should be stated explicitly in the connection agreement, because disputes over "whose fault caused the trip" usually sit exactly at that boundary.

A buyer evaluating an IPP or embedded generator should ask to see, before signing, the plant's maintenance philosophy (planned outage frequency and duration), the metering and settlement process, and how unplanned outages are reported and to what timescale. A [power plant audit](/power-plant-audit-nigeria/) at the point of due diligence, or periodically once supply has started, gives an independent read on whether the generating plant is being run to a standard that supports the availability the contract promises.

## Reliability from the plant side: what to actually measure

Reliability is easy to promise in a sales conversation and hard to verify without data. From the buyer's side, three measures are worth tracking against whatever the supply agreement commits to:

| Measure | What it tells the buyer | How it is usually tracked |
|---|---|---|
| Availability (per cent of contracted hours supplied) | Whether the IPP is meeting its committed uptime | Metered kWh against contracted capacity over a billing period |
| Planned outage notice | Whether maintenance is scheduled with enough warning to protect production | Written notice period in the connection agreement |
| Unplanned trip frequency | Underlying plant health and maintenance discipline | Trip log cross-checked against the buyer's own outage records |

Where an IPP's contracted capacity regularly falls short of demand, a factory usually still needs its own generator or genset fleet as backup or top-up. In that case the total cost of running that backup capacity alongside the IPP tariff is what determines whether the arrangement is actually cheaper than self-generation. The cost of downtime while that gap is being resolved is often the bigger number: see our note on [the true cost of plant downtime per hour](/blog/plant-downtime-cost-per-hour/) for a way to put a figure on it that holds up in a board conversation.

## Where captive power and permits fit in

A factory that decides to generate for its own consumption rather than buy from an IPP is operating under a different part of the same framework: captive power generation. NERC's captive generation route lets a facility generate purely for its own use, and its permit and registration requirements differ from a full generation licence depending on installed capacity. We cover what that permit involves and when it applies in our [guide to the captive power permit in Nigeria](/blog/captive-power-permit-nigeria/), and the broader shift in how state and federal regulators treat self-generation and embedded generation is covered in our [summary of the Electricity Act 2023 for industrial users](/blog/electricity-act-2023-industrial-users/).

The choice between buying from an embedded IPP, going captive, or running a hybrid of both is a load and risk decision specific to each site, not a rule of thumb. A power plant audit that models the site's actual load profile against both options is the fastest way to see which one holds up once fuel price, maintenance cost and outage risk are all put on the same table. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is an embedded generator the same thing as an IPP?

Not quite. Embedded generation is one of the three licence categories an IPP can hold, defined by where the plant connects (the distribution network rather than the transmission grid). An IPP can also hold an on-grid or off-grid licence, so "embedded" describes the connection type, while "IPP" describes the ownership structure.

### Does a factory need NERC's approval to buy power from an IPP?

The IPP itself needs a NERC generation licence to build and operate the plant and to sell power. The buying facility does not need its own generation licence to purchase power under a supply agreement, but it should confirm the seller's licence status and category before signing, since an unlicensed supply arrangement carries regulatory risk for both sides.

### How is the tariff for IPP power usually structured?

Tariffs are negotiated per project rather than published, and they typically track the IPP's fuel cost, which for diesel and gas plants moves with the exchange rate and fuel market. A buyer should ask for a tariff formula tied to a stated fuel index rather than accepting a fixed figure that ignores how fuel costs move.

### What happens if the IPP cannot supply the contracted capacity?

That depends entirely on the connection agreement. Some agreements include compensation or tariff adjustment for shortfall against a stated availability guarantee, others do not. This is one of the clauses worth having reviewed before signing, alongside the metering and outage notice terms.

### Can an industrial cluster have more than one IPP supplying it?

Yes, and larger estates sometimes do, particularly where one embedded generator supplies base load and a second, or the estate's own standby fleet, covers peak demand or acts as backup during planned maintenance. Each supply source still needs its own metering and settlement arrangement with the offtakers it serves.
