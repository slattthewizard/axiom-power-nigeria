# Boiler Water Treatment: Preventing Scale, Corrosion and Carryover in Industrial Boilers

Most boilers don't die from one dramatic event. They die slowly, a millimetre of scale at a time, or through a few small pits that nobody noticed until a tube let go. Almost every one of those failures traces back to the water. The steel, the burners and the controls get the attention, but the water chemistry decides how long the pressure parts last.

This guide covers what boiler water treatment actually does, how external and internal treatment fit together, what blowdown is for, and which tests belong on the daily log sheet. If you run a steam plant in Nigeria, the source water problem comes first, so we'll start there.

## Why boiler water needs treatment at all

Water is a good solvent and a poor boiler fluid. When it turns to steam it leaves everything dissolved in it behind, so the concentration of salts in the drum climbs the longer the boiler runs. Left alone, that causes four separate problems.

Scale is the one everybody knows. Hardness salts (calcium and magnesium) and silica come out of solution on the hottest surfaces and bake into a hard layer. Scale conducts heat far worse than steel, so the tube metal runs hotter than it was designed to. You burn more fuel for the same steam, and eventually the tube overheats, blisters or ruptures.

Oxygen corrosion is the second. Dissolved oxygen in feed water attacks steel at the feed line, economiser and drum, leaving deep, sharp pits. Pitting is nasty because the wall can look fine while one pit goes through.

Caustic attack is the third. If alkalinity is allowed to concentrate under a deposit, the local chemistry becomes aggressive enough to dissolve the protective magnetite layer on the steel. Gouging and cracking follow, usually under scale where nobody can see it.

Carryover is the fourth, and it's the one that reaches beyond the boiler. Foaming, high dissolved solids and violent drum level swings push droplets of boiler water into the steam. Those droplets carry salts, which deposit on superheater tubes and on turbine blades. If your steam goes to a turbine, carryover is how a water problem becomes a rotor problem. The deposits unbalance the rotor, roughen the blade surfaces and, in bad cases, start the erosion and corrosion damage we describe in [turbine blade damage](/blog/turbine-blade-damage/).

## Start with a source water analysis

Before choosing any chemical or any plant, get a proper analysis of the water you're feeding the system. Nigerian sites draw from very different sources: boreholes, municipal supply, surface water, tankered water, sometimes a mix that changes with the season. Hardness, alkalinity, silica, chlorides, iron and suspended solids vary a lot from one site to the next, and sometimes between two boreholes on the same compound.

A treatment programme copied from another plant is a guess. A water that's soft and low in silica needs a very different approach from one that's hard and iron-bearing. Sample at the actual point of supply, use a competent laboratory, and repeat the analysis if the source changes. Honestly, this one page of lab results saves more money than any chemical you'll buy later.

## External treatment: fixing the water before it reaches the boiler

External treatment removes the problem materials from make-up water. Which steps you need depends on the analysis and on boiler pressure.

Filtration comes first. Sand or multimedia filters take out suspended solids, and activated carbon removes chlorine and organics that can damage downstream resins and membranes.

Softening swaps hardness ions for sodium in an ion exchange resin bed. It's the workhorse for low-pressure boilers on moderately hard water. The resin needs regeneration with brine, and the hardness test on the softener outlet tells you when it's exhausted. A softener that's quietly gone past its run length is one of the commonest causes of sudden scale.

Reverse osmosis pushes water through a membrane that rejects most dissolved salts. It cuts the total dissolved solids going to the boiler, which lowers blowdown demand. It does need good pretreatment, because membranes foul and scale, and it needs someone who actually monitors pressure drops and cleans on time.

Demineralisation uses cation and anion resin beds (sometimes with mixed beds as a polisher) to strip almost all ions, including silica. Higher-pressure plants and any boiler feeding a turbine usually need this level of purity. It costs more to run, but so does a turbine overhaul.

Deaeration removes dissolved oxygen and carbon dioxide mechanically. Feed water is heated, usually with steam, and sprayed or cascaded so the gases come out and vent. A working deaerator takes most of the oxygen out before any chemical is added. A deaerator that's running cold or has a blocked vent pushes the whole oxygen load onto your scavenger and the feed line.

## Internal treatment: chemistry inside the system

Even good external treatment leaves traces. These are the boiler water treatment chemicals you'll meet most often.

Oxygen scavengers react with whatever oxygen survives deaeration. Sulphite based products are common on lower-pressure plants. Organic scavengers are used where you don't want to add dissolved solids to the boiler water. Whichever you use, dose it by the residual measured in the feed water or boiler water, not by habit.

Phosphate programmes precipitate any residual hardness as a soft sludge that can be removed by blowdown instead of baking onto tubes. They also help set the pH. Phosphate dosing needs steady control, because over-dosing can itself create deposits.

Alkalinity control keeps the boiler water in a pH range where steel stays protected by its magnetite film. Too low and you get acidic corrosion. Too high and you risk caustic attack and foaming. The right range depends on the boiler design and operating pressure, so take it from the boiler maker, not from a generic chart.

Condensate line treatment is a separate job. Steam condenses back to water carrying carbon dioxide, which makes carbonic acid and eats condensate pipework, usually as grooving along the bottom of the pipe. Neutralising amines raise the pH of the condensate and counter the acid. Filming amines lay down a thin protective layer on the metal surface. Which suits you depends on the condensate system and what the steam touches, so check the choice with your chemical supplier and the boiler maker.

## Boiler blowdown: getting the concentrated stuff out

Treatment keeps impurities from sticking. Blowdown removes them. The two types do different jobs.

Continuous blowdown takes a small, steady flow from near the water surface, where dissolved solids are most concentrated. It controls the total dissolved solids, and it's usually adjusted by a valve or an automatic controller reading conductivity.

Bottom blowdown is a short, manual or timed discharge from the lowest point of the drum or mud drum. It removes the settled sludge and precipitated solids. It's done in bursts, on a schedule, and the sequence matters. Open the valves in the order your operating procedure specifies, never leave the line unattended and never run it with the boiler low on water.

Blowdown has a cost. Every litre you discharge takes treated water and heat with it. Too little and the solids concentrate until you get scale, foaming and carryover. Too much and you waste fuel and chemicals. The balance point comes from the conductivity limit set for your boiler, and it moves when the make-up water quality moves. That's another reason to track the source water.

Safety note: blowdown valves, sample coolers and dosing lines are live pressure and hot-water equipment. Hands-on work needs isolation, lockout-tagout and a permit to work, and chemical handling needs correct PPE and the supplier's safety data sheets.

## What to test every day, and what each test protects against

A treatment programme without a test routine is just a hope. Record results every shift or day and watch the trend. The tests below are the usual daily set. The limits for each come from the boiler manufacturer and the operating pressure, and your treatment supplier should confirm them in writing for your plant. We aren't quoting numbers here because a value that's right for one boiler can be wrong for another.

| Parameter | Where it's measured | What it protects against |
|---|---|---|
| Conductivity or TDS | Boiler water, feed water, condensate | Carryover, foaming, excessive solids concentration. Also drives the blowdown rate |
| pH | Feed water, boiler water, condensate | Acidic corrosion at low values, caustic attack and foaming at high values |
| Hardness | Softener outlet, feed water | Scale on heat transfer surfaces. Any reading above zero on soft water means a problem upstream |
| Dissolved oxygen | Deaerator outlet, feed water | Oxygen pitting in feed lines, economiser and drum |
| Scavenger residual | Boiler water or feed water | Confirms the oxygen scavenger is actually present |
| Phosphate residual | Boiler water | Hardness precipitation and pH buffering. Both too little and too much cause trouble |
| Silica | Boiler water, make-up water (high-pressure units) | Silica scale and silica carryover that deposits on turbine blades |
| Condensate pH and iron | Condensate return | Condensate line corrosion and iron pick-up that fouls the boiler |

## Why this matters if you run steam turbines

A boiler with poor water quality hurts itself. A steam plant with poor water quality hurts the turbine too. Silica and salt carryover form deposits on blades and nozzles, reducing efficiency and changing the flow path. Carried-over moisture and corrosive deposits can erode and pit blading. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is boiler water treatment?

It's the combination of external treatment (filtration, softening, reverse osmosis, demineralisation and deaeration) and internal treatment (chemical dosing and blowdown) that keeps boiler water from forming scale, corroding steel or carrying impurities into the steam. The right mix depends on your source water, boiler pressure and what the steam is used for.

### How often should boiler water be tested?

Key parameters such as conductivity, pH, hardness and scavenger residual are normally checked at least daily, and many plants test every shift. High-pressure boilers and those feeding turbines need closer monitoring, often with online analysers. Your boiler maker and treatment supplier should set the schedule and the limits for your specific plant.

### What is the difference between continuous and bottom blowdown?

Continuous blowdown is a small steady bleed from near the water surface that controls dissolved solids, usually by conductivity. Bottom blowdown is a short, periodic discharge from the lowest point of the boiler that clears settled sludge. You normally need both, and neither replaces the other.

### Can I use the same treatment chemicals as another plant?

Not safely without checking. Chemical choice and dose depend on the water analysis, the boiler design, the operating pressure and the steam use. A programme that works elsewhere can cause deposits or corrosion on your boiler if the water or conditions differ. Get a source water analysis and take advice from your boiler maker and treatment supplier first.
