# Centrifugal Pump Working Principle, Common Problems and Maintenance

A centrifugal pump that trips, loses flow or starts vibrating on a Nigerian factory floor rarely fails without warning. Bearings run hot before they seize, seals weep before they let go, and flow drops off before a pump stops delivering altogether. The problem is that most of these signs get noticed only after production has already been interrupted, because the people running the plant were never shown what is actually happening inside the casing.

This guide sets out the centrifugal pump working principle in plain terms, the parts that do the work, and the failure modes that account for most unplanned pump downtime on Nigerian sites: cavitation, seal leaks, bearing failure and recirculation. It closes with a short note on condition monitoring, because most of these problems can be caught weeks before they cause a stoppage.

## How a Centrifugal Pump Works

A centrifugal pump moves liquid by spinning it, not by squeezing it the way a positive displacement pump does. Liquid enters at the centre (the eye) of a rotating impeller. The impeller's vanes accelerate that liquid outward, giving it kinetic energy. As the fast-moving liquid leaves the impeller and enters the volute or diffuser casing, the flow path widens and slows, and that kinetic energy converts into pressure energy. The result is liquid leaving the discharge nozzle at higher pressure than it entered the suction nozzle.

Two things follow from this principle that matter on a real site. First, a centrifugal pump needs liquid at its eye to work at all. It cannot push air the way a positive displacement pump can, which is why priming and adequate suction conditions matter more here than on almost any other rotating machine. Second, head and flow move in opposite directions on a fixed-speed pump: push more flow through it and the head it can generate falls, and vice versa. That relationship is drawn as the pump curve, and it decides almost everything about how well the pump performs once it is installed.

## Main Parts of a Centrifugal Pump

The parts below appear, in some form, on nearly every centrifugal pump found in a Nigerian plant, whether it is moving process water, effluent, cooling water or a chemical duty.

- **Casing**: contains the pumped liquid and houses the volute or diffuser passages that convert velocity to pressure.
- **Impeller**: the rotating component with vanes that does the actual work on the liquid; can be open, semi-open or closed depending on the duty.
- **Shaft**: transmits torque from the driver (usually an electric motor) to the impeller.
- **Bearings**: support the shaft radially and axially and take up the hydraulic thrust generated by the impeller.
- **Mechanical seal or packing**: prevents leakage where the rotating shaft passes through the stationary casing.
- **Wear rings**: close running clearances between the impeller and casing; their condition governs internal recirculation losses as the pump ages.
- **Baseplate and coupling**: hold pump and motor in alignment; alignment error here is one of the most common root causes behind seal and bearing complaints.

## Operating on the Curve: Why the Best Efficiency Point Matters

Every centrifugal pump has a manufacturer's performance curve plotting head against flow at a given speed and impeller diameter. Somewhere on that curve sits the best efficiency point (BEP): the flow at which the pump converts the most input power into useful hydraulic work, with the least internal turbulence.

A pump running close to its BEP tends to run quietly, cool and for a long service life. A pump running well to the left of the BEP (low flow relative to its curve) or well to the right (high flow) behaves differently. To the left, recirculation inside the impeller eye and at the volute tip increases, generating localised low pressure zones, vibration and, at the extreme, cavitation-type damage even where suction conditions are otherwise fine. To the right, the pump can run into a region where available suction head runs short of what the impeller needs, and radial thrust on the shaft rises, loading bearings and seals harder than the design intended.

The practical point for a maintenance manager is that a pump problem is often, at root, an operating point problem. A throttled discharge valve, an undersized pipe run, a system that has been re-piped since commissioning, or a control set point that no longer matches the process can all push a pump off its curve long before any single part actually breaks. Checking where the pump is actually operating against its curve is one of the cheaper diagnostic steps available, and it belongs early in any pump investigation, not as an afterthought once parts have already been condemned.

## Common Centrifugal Pump Problems

Four failure modes account for most of the unplanned centrifugal pump downtime seen on industrial and facility sites: cavitation, mechanical seal leakage, bearing failure and internal recirculation. The table below is a starting point for triage, not a substitute for a proper inspection.

| Symptom | Likely cause | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| Crackling or gravel-like noise, erratic gauge readings | Cavitation from insufficient suction head (NPSH shortfall) | Suction strainer condition, suction lift and pipe losses, liquid temperature, NPSH available versus NPSH required on the curve |
| Steady drip or mist at the seal, visible wear on the seal faces | Mechanical seal wear from dry running, misalignment or a wrong flush plan | Shaft and coupling alignment, seal flush line, seal chamber pressure, seal history against duty |
| Bearing housing running hot, metallic smell, rising vibration at bearing frequencies | Bearing failure from lubrication fault, misalignment or unbalance | Oil or grease condition and quantity, vibration spectrum, coupling alignment, shaft runout |
| Reduced flow or pressure that was not there at commissioning | Impeller or wear ring erosion, or the pump running away from its curve | Current draw against rated, discharge pressure against the curve at that flow, internal clearances at the next opportunity |
| Vibration that rises at low flow and falls as flow increases | Internal recirculation from operating left of the best efficiency point | Actual operating point against the curve, minimum continuous stable flow rating, control valve position |

A pump exhibiting more than one of these signs at once is usually telling the maintenance team that the root cause sits upstream of the part that is visibly failing. A seal that keeps failing on the same pump, for instance, is rarely a seal quality problem on its own; it is worth checking alignment and the operating point before ordering another seal kit, which is exactly the kind of investigation covered in more depth in our note on [mechanical seal failure](/blog/mechanical-seal-failure/). Cavitation specifically, including how to distinguish NPSH shortfall from other causes of pump noise, is covered in its own detail in our article on [pump cavitation](/blog/pump-cavitation/).

## Condition Monitoring That Catches These Faults Early

Every one of the failure modes above gives measurable warning before it causes a stoppage, provided someone is taking the measurements. A basic condition monitoring programme for centrifugal pumps typically covers:

Vibration readings taken at defined points on the bearing housings, trended over time rather than judged against a single reading. A rising trend, even within an apparently acceptable overall level, is often the earliest sign of bearing wear, unbalance or a developing misalignment. The same vibration discipline that catches early rotor faults on a steam turbine, discussed in our piece on [steam turbine high vibration](/blog/steam-turbine-high-vibration/), applies directly to pump bearings.

Oil or grease sampling on pumps with an oil-lubricated bearing housing, checking for water contamination, particle count and viscosity change. The same analysis discipline used on turbine lubricating oil, set out in our article on [turbine lube oil analysis](/blog/turbine-lube-oil-analysis/), transfers well to pump bearing housings running on circulating or bath lubrication.

Thermography on bearing housings and motor terminals, which picks up heat that a hand check will miss until it is already serious.

Periodic verification of the operating point against the pump curve, particularly after any change to the piping, control strategy or duty.

Building this into a fixed schedule, rather than reacting only when a pump audibly struggles, is what keeps a rotating equipment fleet from generating the kind of surprise stoppage that is expensive to absorb; our note on [plant downtime cost per hour](/blog/plant-downtime-cost-per-hour/) sets out why that cost is usually larger than the repair itself once lost production is counted in.

## Getting a Proper Diagnosis Before Ordering Parts

Replacing a seal or a bearing without first establishing why the previous one failed tends to produce the same failure again, often within months. A structured assessment, covering alignment, operating point, suction conditions and bearing lubrication history, generally costs far less than a repeat failure and the production loss that comes with it. Where isolation, electrical work or work on a pressurised system is involved, that assessment and any follow-on work should always be carried out under lockout and tagout by qualified personnel with a permit to work; this is not a task for informal intervention on a running machine.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the working principle of a centrifugal pump?

A centrifugal pump uses a rotating impeller to accelerate liquid outward from its centre, giving the liquid kinetic energy. As that fast-moving liquid passes into the widening volute or diffuser casing, its velocity drops and converts into pressure, which is what pushes the liquid out at the discharge. It depends on liquid, not air, filling the impeller eye to work correctly.

### What causes most centrifugal pump problems on Nigerian industrial sites?

The recurring causes are insufficient suction head leading to cavitation, misalignment or a wrong flush plan wearing out mechanical seals, lubrication faults or unbalance damaging bearings, and pumps operating well away from their best efficiency point because of throttled valves or a piping system that has changed since commissioning. Heat, dust and inconsistent power supply add to wear rates but rarely start the failure on their own.

### How do I know if my pump is running off its best efficiency point?

Compare the pump's actual flow and head, read from field instruments, against the manufacturer's curve for that impeller diameter and speed. If the operating point sits well to the left or right of the marked best efficiency point, expect higher vibration, reduced bearing and seal life, and in some cases symptoms that look like cavitation even when suction conditions are adequate.

### Can a centrifugal pump run dry?

Running dry, even briefly, is one of the fastest ways to damage a mechanical seal and, on some designs, the wear rings and bearings, because the liquid that normally cools and lubricates these components is absent. A pump should never be started or left running without confirmed liquid at the suction, and any suction-side isolation or level control that could allow this should be checked as part of routine maintenance.

### How often should centrifugal pumps be inspected in an industrial plant?

Frequency depends on duty, criticality and the manufacturer's own guidance, but a typical pattern on a continuously running industrial pump includes routine operator checks daily, vibration and temperature readings on a fixed interval such as monthly, and a fuller inspection covering alignment, seal condition and internal clearances on a longer cycle or at the first sign of a trending fault. The OEM manual for the specific pump should always be treated as the governing document for interval and tolerance values.
