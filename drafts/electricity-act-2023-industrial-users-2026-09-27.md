# Electricity Act 2023: What It Means for Industrial Power Users in Nigeria

If your plant runs its own generators, buys from an independent power producer, or is weighing a captive power plant, the Electricity Act 2023 changed some of the ground rules you are operating on. The Act, signed into law on 9 June 2023, repealed the Electricity Power Sector Reform Act 2005 and rewrote how electricity generation, transmission, distribution and regulation work in Nigeria, at both the federal and state level.

For a plant manager or procurement officer, most of the headlines about the Act (state electricity markets, mini-grids, renewable energy targets) sit some distance from the daily job of keeping a factory or facility powered. This article sets out the parts of the Act that actually touch industrial self-generation, captive power and cross-border supply arrangements, based on what is published by the Act itself and by the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission (NERC). It is a summary for planning purposes, not legal advice: confirm the current position with your own counsel or with NERC before you commit to a structure.

## Why the Electricity Act 2023 exists

Before 2023, electricity regulation in Nigeria ran almost entirely through federal law and the old NERC framework built on the 2005 Reform Act. States had a constrained role, and most on-site generation and third-party power arrangements were assessed against a single national rulebook. The Electricity Act 2023 replaced that framework with one that gives states their own constitutional lane to regulate electricity within their borders, while keeping NERC as the federal regulator for the national grid, cross-state trading and matters states have not yet taken on themselves.

For a plant that generates its own power, buys from an embedded generator, or is considering a captive plant, the practical effect is that the applicable rulebook now depends partly on where the facility sits and whether that state has stood up its own electricity market.

## State electricity markets: what changed for industrial users

Sections 224 to 230 of the Act allow a state to create and regulate its own intrastate electricity market, covering generation, transmission, distribution, trading and supply within that state, provided the activity does not rely on the federal transmission grid. A state that exercises this power sets up its own regulatory commission and can license generation, distribution and supply directly, instead of routing every licence through NERC.

Lagos State moved first, enacting the Lagos State Electricity Law 2024 in April 2024 and establishing the Lagos State Electricity Regulatory Commission (LASERC). Other states, including Rivers, Kano and Ogun, have signalled intent to set up their own markets, though at different stages of legislative and institutional readiness. For a facility in Lagos, Port Harcourt, Kano or elsewhere, this matters because a licensing question that used to have one answer nationwide can now have a different answer depending on the state, and on how far that state's own electricity law has progressed. Before you sign a supply agreement with an embedded generator or IPP, check which regulator (state or NERC) actually has jurisdiction over that arrangement in that location.

## Captive and embedded generation under the Act

For an industrial site, this is the section of the Act with the most direct bearing. NERC's regulation for captive power generation, issued under the Act, sets a threshold at 1 MW of installed capacity for self-consumption:

- Below 1 MW, generating electricity solely for your own use is exempt from a full generation licence, but the enterprise must still register the plant with NERC (or the relevant state commission, where that state has an established market).
- At or above 1 MW, self-consumption generation needs a Captive Power Generation Permit.
- Either way, a captive permit or registration covers generation for your own use only. It does not authorise selling surplus power to a third party; that needs a separate licence class, whether trading, embedded generation, or distribution.

This is the same regulatory lane that /blog/captive-power-permit-nigeria/ covers in more detail on the permit application itself. The Act also gives NERC a mandate to license embedded generation, hybridised generation and co-generation, which is the framework an industrial cluster or estate would use if it wanted to generate on-site and sell to neighbouring plants rather than only to itself.

None of this changes the engineering side of a captive plant: sizing, fuel choice, and maintenance regime are still decided by load, reliability targets and total cost of ownership, not by the permit class. What the Act changes is the compliance step before you commission the plant, and who you file with.

## Mini-grids and the industrial estate angle

The Act also carries a mini-grid licensing framework, aimed mainly at off-grid and underserved-area supply rather than at large industrial consumers directly. It is relevant to industrial users mainly at the estate or cluster level: an industrial park or free trade zone that wants to run its own local distribution network, buying bulk power from a captive plant or IPP and reselling it to tenant factories, sits inside this mini-grid and embedded-distribution regime rather than the captive-generation rules above. If your facility sits inside, or is considering joining, an estate-level power arrangement, the licence held by the estate operator (mini-grid, embedded distribution, or trading) is worth confirming before you rely on it for supply continuity.

## Captive generation tiers at a glance

| Arrangement | Typical licensing position under the Act | What it does not cover |
|---|---|---|
| Self-generation below 1 MW, own use only | Exempt from full generation licence; registration with NERC or relevant state commission still required | Sale of any surplus power to a third party |
| Self-generation at or above 1 MW, own use only | Captive Power Generation Permit from NERC (or state commission where applicable) | Sale of surplus power; supply to other sites without a separate licence |
| Embedded or co-generation with surplus sold to nearby users | Separate embedded generation or trading licence class | Automatic rights across state lines, where a state market applies |
| Estate or park-level local distribution and resale | Mini-grid or embedded distribution licence held by the estate operator | Direct licensing relationship between NERC and individual tenant plants |

This table is a planning summary, not a substitute for confirming your specific case against the current regulations, which NERC updates from time to time.

## What this means for your generation strategy

Regulation is one input into a captive power decision, not the whole of it. A plant that is already running diesel or gas generation for reliability reasons, and is weighing whether to formalise that into a larger captive plant or an IPP arrangement, still has to work through the same questions covered elsewhere on this site: what a realistic cost per kWh looks like once fuel, maintenance and capital are accounted for, and what an hour of unplanned downtime actually costs against the alternative of paying for more resilient generation. The Act changes the licensing path for a captive plant; it does not change the arithmetic behind sizing it correctly or maintaining it to the interval the OEM specifies.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the Electricity Act 2023 mean I need a permit for my existing generator?

It depends on capacity and use. If your generator (or generator fleet) produces electricity solely for your own site and stays below 1 MW combined installed capacity, current NERC guidance treats it as exempt from a full generation licence but still requiring registration. Above 1 MW for self-consumption, a Captive Power Generation Permit applies. Confirm your specific installed capacity and arrangement with NERC or your state commission, since thresholds and guidance can be updated.

### Can my state's own electricity law override NERC for my plant?

Where a state has validly established its own electricity market under the Act, that state's regulatory commission can take on licensing for generation, distribution, trading and supply within the state, provided the activity does not use the federal transmission grid. NERC continues to regulate the national grid and cross-state matters, and states that have not stood up their own market. Which regulator applies to your facility depends on your state's stage of implementation, so it is worth checking directly rather than assuming.

### Does the Act let me sell excess power from my captive generator to another factory nearby?

A captive generation registration or permit covers electricity produced for your own consumption. Selling surplus to a third party, even a neighbouring facility, sits under a different licence class, such as embedded generation or trading, and needs its own authorisation. Treat captive and resale as two separate regulatory questions when you plan capacity.

### Is diesel or gas self-generation still worth it under the new rules?

The Act changes the compliance path, not the underlying economics of running your own generation. Whether self-generation makes sense still comes down to your load profile, fuel cost and availability, reliability requirements, and the maintenance regime you can sustain. A plant that already relies on diesel or gas generation for reliability reasons is unlikely to see that rationale change because of the Act; what changes is which registration or permit that generation now sits under.

### Where can I find the current, authoritative text of the requirements?

The Electricity Act 2023 itself and NERC's published regulations, including the regulation for captive power generation, are the primary sources. State electricity laws, where a state has enacted one, sit alongside these. Because implementation is still progressing state by state, confirm the current requirements for your location directly with NERC or the relevant state commission rather than relying on a general summary.
