# Mechanical Seal Failure: Causes, What the Damage Tells You, and How to Prevent Repeat Failures

A pump that keeps losing its seal every few months is not unlucky. Mechanical seal failure almost always traces back to one of a short list of causes: dry running, misalignment, a flush arrangement that does not suit the fluid, or contamination getting between the faces. Chasing each leak as a one-off repair without asking why the seal died is how a plant ends up replacing the same seal three or four times a year.

For a maintenance manager or plant engineer running centrifugal pumps in a factory, water treatment plant, refinery or gas-to-power facility, the seal is usually the first thing to fail on a pump that is otherwise sound. Understanding how the seal actually works, and what a failed one is trying to tell you, turns a recurring leak into a fixable root cause rather than a recurring parts order.

This article covers how mechanical seals work, the common failure causes on Nigerian sites, how to read the damage on a returned seal, API 682 flush plans in general terms, and what a realistic prevention programme looks like.

## How a Mechanical Seal Works

A mechanical seal stops process fluid escaping along a rotating shaft by running two flat faces against each other: one fixed to the pump housing (the stationary face), one fixed to the shaft (the rotating face). Springs or a bellows hold the faces together with light contact pressure, and the process fluid, or a separate flush fluid, forms a thin film between them that lubricates the faces and carries away frictional heat.

That film is typically only a few microns thick. The seal is not designed to run dry, even briefly, because without the film the faces generate heat fast enough to damage the seal in seconds to minutes, not hours. This is the single fact that explains most seal failures on site: something interrupted the film, or changed its temperature or chemistry enough that it stopped doing its job.

Most industrial pumps today use cartridge seals rather than component seals fitted piece by piece on the shaft. A cartridge seal arrives pre-assembled on its own sleeve with a setting clip, so it drops onto the shaft as one unit and is set to the correct compression without measuring individual spring lengths. This cuts fitting error, which is one of the more common causes of early seal failure on sites where a seal is changed by whoever is available rather than by someone trained on that seal type.

## The Main Causes of Mechanical Seal Failure

### Dry running and vapourisation

If the seal chamber runs low on fluid, or the fluid flashes to vapour at the faces because of low suction pressure or high temperature, the lubricating film disappears and the faces run metal to metal (or hard-face to hard-face). This is the fastest way to destroy a seal, often within minutes, and it is common on pumps that have been run against a closed valve, run with air in the casing after a restart, or starved by a suction problem such as a clogged strainer or low tank level.

### Misalignment and shaft movement

A mechanical seal needs the shaft to run true and the pump and driver to be aligned within the coupling manufacturer's tolerance. Misalignment, a worn coupling, bent shaft or worn bearings all show up at the seal faces as uneven wear, chipping on one side of the face, or a seal that leaks intermittently as the shaft whips. On many sites the seal takes the blame for a fault that actually sits in the bearings or coupling.

### Wrong or blocked flush arrangement

The flush plan supplies clean, cool fluid to the seal faces, either drawn from the pump's own discharge or from an external source. If the flush line is undersized, blocked by debris, or was never designed for the duty (for example, a plan meant for clean water fitted to a line now running fluid with solids), the faces run hot and dry, and failure follows. Changes to the process, such as a new duty point or a different product through the same pump, can turn a previously adequate flush plan into an inadequate one without anyone changing a line.

### Contamination and abrasive wear

Grit, scale, rust and process solids act like grinding paste between the faces. On sites drawing from open channels, boreholes, cooling towers or ponds, or wherever housekeeping around the pump is poor, abrasive contamination is one of the most common seal killers. It shows as scoring or grooving across the face rather than the even wear pattern of a healthy seal.

### Chemical attack and elastomer failure

The O-rings, gaskets and elastomer components in a seal must be compatible with the process fluid and its temperature. A seal fitted with the wrong elastomer for a chemical dosing line, or one exposed to a cleaning chemical it was never rated for, can swell, harden or crack even while the faces themselves look fine.

### Pressure and temperature excursions

A seal is rated for a pressure and temperature envelope. Running a pump against a closed discharge, a steam trace failure that lets a hot line cool and then thermal-shock on restart, or a pressure spike from a valve closing too fast, can push the seal outside that envelope and crack a face or distort the seal ring.

## Reading a Failed Seal

A returned seal carries a record of how it died, and looking at it before throwing it away is worth the five minutes it takes.

| What you see | Likely cause | What to check next |
|---|---|---|
| Even, dull wear ring across the whole face | Normal wear, seal at end of life | Nothing wrong, plan the replacement interval |
| Heat cracking, discolouration, blistered elastomer | Dry running or flash of process fluid | Suction conditions, minimum flow, flush line blockage |
| Chipped or cracked face, uneven contact pattern | Misalignment or shaft runout | Coupling alignment, bearing condition, shaft straightness |
| Fine circumferential scoring or grooving | Abrasive contamination in the flush or process fluid | Flush filtration, strainer condition, upstream housekeeping |
| Swollen, softened or brittle O-rings | Chemical incompatibility | Elastomer material against the actual fluid and temperature, not the design fluid |
| Face separation with visible gap marks | Excess axial shaft movement or wrong spring compression | Bearing end play, correct cartridge setting on install |

Where the failure involves rotating equipment that is still coupled to its driver, or where diagnosis means removing guards or breaking into a pressurised line, that work needs qualified personnel with lockout/tagout and a permit to work, not a quick look while the pump is still running.

## Seal Flush Plans in General Terms

API 682 sets out standard piping arrangements, called flush plans, that describe how fluid is supplied to and around the seal faces. Sites do not need to memorise the full standard, but understanding the logic of the common plans helps when reviewing why a seal keeps failing.

Single-seal arrangements typically use one of a small number of plans. Plan 11 takes fluid from the pump's own discharge through a restriction orifice and routes it back into the seal chamber, which is the default choice where the process fluid itself is clean enough to lubricate the faces. Plan 21 and Plan 23 add a cooler into that recirculation loop for hotter services, with Plan 23 using a pumping ring to circulate fluid through the cooler more efficiently than Plan 21's simple recirculation. Plan 32 injects a clean, compatible fluid from an external source directly into the seal chamber, which is the usual fix when the process fluid itself is abrasive, prone to polymerising, or otherwise unsuitable for lubricating the faces.

Dual-seal arrangements are used where a single seal cannot be trusted to contain the fluid safely, such as toxic, flammable or environmentally sensitive services. Plan 52 supplies an unpressurised buffer fluid between two seals arranged back to back or tandem. Plan 53 supplies a pressurised barrier fluid between two seals so that any leakage path runs from the barrier fluid into the process, rather than the other way round.

None of this replaces the pump and seal manufacturer's own documentation. The specific plan, orifice size, cooler duty and instrumentation for a given seal on a given pump come from the OEM data sheet for that machine, and that is what governs when a flush arrangement is being reviewed or changed. ## Prevention: What Actually Cuts Repeat Failures

Take a hypothetical example: a plant is replacing the seal on a cooling water pump roughly every four months, well short of the one to three years a correctly applied cartridge seal on clean service should manage. Before ordering another seal, the fault-finding sequence should look at, in order: suction conditions at start-up (air, low level, closed valve), alignment and bearing condition, flush line condition and filtration, and whether the elastomer and face materials actually match the fluid the pump is now running rather than the fluid it was specified for years earlier.

A short list of habits removes most repeat failures:

- Never start or run a pump against a closed discharge valve, and confirm minimum continuous flow for the duty.
- Check coupling alignment on a set schedule, not only after a seal fails.
- Keep flush lines and strainers on a cleaning or inspection interval, especially on sites where fluid quality varies with the season.
- Record every seal failure with the cause, not just "seal replaced", so a pattern across a fleet of pumps becomes visible.
- Confirm elastomer and face material selection whenever the process fluid, additive package or operating temperature changes, rather than assuming the original specification still applies.

If a seal keeps failing on the same pump despite good practice on the points above, the underlying issue is often in the pump's operating point, its foundation, or a flush arrangement that was never matched to the actual duty. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a mechanical seal last?

There is no single number, because it depends heavily on the fluid, duty and how well the pump is aligned and run. As a general guide, a correctly applied seal on a clean, compatible duty with good alignment can run for one to three years between failures, while a poorly matched seal on a dirty or hot duty may fail within weeks. The pattern of failures across a fleet tells you more than any single seal's life.

### Can a mechanical seal be repaired instead of replaced?

Some seal designs allow the faces or elastomers to be replaced as a repair kit rather than buying a whole new cartridge, but this depends on the seal type, the extent of damage and whether the sleeve and hardware are still within tolerance. A seal with a cracked or heavily worn face is usually not worth repairing against the cost and risk of a leak recurring soon after.

### What is the difference between a component seal and a cartridge seal?

A component seal is fitted piece by piece directly on the pump shaft, with spring compression set by measurement during installation, which leaves room for fitting error. A cartridge seal comes pre-assembled on its own sleeve with the compression already set, so it installs as a single unit with far less chance of a setup mistake, which is why most industrial pumps now specify cartridge seals as standard.

### Why does a new seal fail again within a few weeks of replacement?

A quick repeat failure almost always points to a root cause that was not fixed the first time, such as misalignment, dry running at start-up, or a flush line still blocked or undersized. Simply swapping the seal without checking these factors tends to produce another failure on roughly the same timescale as the last one.

### Does the seal flush plan need to be reviewed if nothing about the pump has changed?

It is worth reviewing if the process fluid, its temperature, or its solids content has changed even slightly, since a flush plan that suited the original duty can become inadequate for a different one without any change to the pump itself. A flush plan review is also worthwhile whenever a seal is failing repeatedly for no obvious mechanical reason.
