# Borescope Inspection on Gas Turbines: What It Finds and When to Schedule One

A plant that shuts a gas turbine down for a full hot gas path inspection without first running a borescope has usually spent money finding out what a borescope inspection would have told it in a day. A borescope inspection lets an engineer look inside the combustor, hot gas path and compressor of a running or recently shut down machine without pulling a single casing joint, and it is the single cheapest way to know whether a unit can keep running to its next planned outage or needs to come off line sooner.

For a plant manager scheduling an outage window, or a maintenance manager trying to justify one, the borescope result is usually the deciding evidence. It does not replace a hot gas path inspection or a major overhaul, but it tells you whether one is still months away or already overdue.

This guide covers what a borescope inspection actually looks at on an industrial gas turbine, where the access ports sit, what the common findings mean, and how the same tool is used on large reciprocating engines. It also covers what should be in the report you get back, because a photo album with no findings written down is close to useless when it is time to plan the next outage.

## What a borescope inspection is

A borescope is a long, flexible or rigid optical probe with a camera and light source at the tip, small enough to pass through dedicated inspection ports built into the turbine casing. The operator feeds the probe through a port, navigates it past the stationary and rotating rows using the engine's own rotation (bump the turning gear, or rotate by hand on a small unit) and records video and still images of each stage as it passes.

Modern industrial borescopes add measurement functions: a calibrated stereo tip can size a crack or a coating loss area directly on screen, rather than the inspector estimating it against a reference mark. That measurement is what turns a borescope inspection from "we saw something" into a number an engineer can compare against the OEM's limits.

The same tool and the same logic apply to large diesel and gas reciprocating engines used in industrial gensets: a borescope through the injector bore or a dedicated inspection port shows piston crown condition, valve seats, and cylinder liner wear without pulling the head. It is used less often on gensets than on turbines because a head-off inspection is a smaller job, but it is useful when an engine is showing symptoms (see /blog/generator-low-output-causes/) and the plant wants evidence before committing to a strip-down.

## Where borescope inspection sits in the turbine inspection cycle

Heavy-duty industrial gas turbines are typically maintained on a cycle built around three planned inspection levels: a combustion inspection (the shallowest, focused on the combustor hardware and the first stage nozzles it feeds), a hot gas path inspection (the combustor plus the full turbine section: nozzles, blades, shrouds), and a major inspection (the full unit, including the compressor and rotor). OEM manuals set the running hours or start count that trigger each level for a given frame, and a plant's actual interval is governed by that manual, not by a generic rule (see /blog/turbine-inspection-intervals/ for how those intervals are set and adjusted for fuel and duty cycle).

A borescope inspection is not one of those three levels. It is the tool used to check condition between them, and increasingly the tool used to decide whether a scheduled inspection can be pushed out or must be brought forward. A unit approaching its combustion inspection interval, showing no unusual trip history or vibration trend, can have that interval confirmed or extended with a borescope look at the combustor and first stage. A unit that trips unexpectedly (see /blog/gas-turbine-trip-causes/) is a borescope candidate immediately, before the cause is guessed at from control system data alone.

Typical prompts to schedule a borescope inspection, independent of the fixed calendar:

- Approaching a scheduled combustion or hot gas path inspection and deciding whether to extend, hold, or bring it forward
- After an unplanned trip, particularly one flagged as overtemperature, vibration, or flame-related
- After a period of off-design operation: extended part load, frequent starts and stops, or a fuel gas quality event
- Following the harmattan season in northern and central Nigeria, where inlet air dust loading is highest (see /blog/harmattan-dust-turbine-derating/)
- Before a major inspection or overhaul, to scope the work in advance rather than opening the unit to find out (see /blog/turbine-overhaul-scope-of-work/)

## Access ports and what they cover

Every heavy-duty gas turbine frame has a defined set of borescope ports built in by the OEM, typically at the compressor inlet and mid-stages, the combustor liners, and each stage of the turbine section. The port count and location are fixed by design: an inspector cannot improvise a new hole, and the plan for which ports are opened on a given inspection is set before the unit is taken off line so the outage window is not spent deciding where to look.

On most frames, opening the full set of ports and passing the probe through every stage is a half-day to full-day job depending on unit size and the number of findings that need closer photography or measurement. A quick "confidence check" limited to the combustor and first-stage nozzle, which is where most fuel and combustion-related damage shows first, is faster and is often what a plant runs between full inspections.

Safety governs the sequence: the unit must be confirmed stopped, isolated per the plant's lockout and permit to work procedure, and allowed to cool to a safe handling temperature before any port cover comes off, and purge and confined-space precautions apply where the casing or duct work is entered. This is qualified personnel work under a permit, not a job for whoever is on shift with a torch.

## What the inspection is looking for

The findings fall into a small number of categories, and the same categories apply whether the borescope is looking at a combustor liner, a turbine nozzle, a rotating blade, or a compressor stage.

**Coating condition.** Hot gas path components carry thermal barrier and oxidation-resistant coatings. The borescope image shows whether that coating is intact, discoloured (an early sign of overheating or coating degradation), or has spalled away to expose bare base metal. Coating loss on its own is a maintenance planning item; coating loss combined with base metal attack changes the urgency.

**Cracking.** Thermal cycling, particularly frequent starts and trips, drives cracking at cooling holes, trailing edges and other stress concentration points on nozzles and blades. The borescope records crack location, orientation and length. The OEM manual sets the limits for what is monitor-and-return versus what triggers immediate repair or replacement; those limits vary by frame, stage and crack location, so this guide will not quote a length. Whoever reviews the footage needs the specific engine's manual open next to it, not a generic number (see /blog/turbine-bearing-babbitt-failure/ for a comparable component-specific limit problem on the bearing side).

**Foreign object damage (FOD).** Dings, tears and dents on leading edges point to hardware that has come loose upstream, commonly a liner segment, a bolt, or debris ingested through the inlet. Any FOD finding should trigger a look upstream of where it was found, because the source is usually still there.

**Erosion and deposits.** Dust ingestion (a known issue during harmattan in the north of the country) and fuel-related deposits both change component surface finish and, over time, aerodynamic performance. Coking, specifically carbon deposit build-up in and around fuel nozzles and combustor hardware, is a fuel and combustion system issue as much as a hot gas path one, and a borescope finding of heavy coking is usually followed up with a fuel system review rather than a hot section repair alone.

| Finding | What it usually points to | Typical next step |
|---|---|---|
| Coating discolouration, no spallation | Localised overheating, possibly a combustion imbalance | Monitor, cross-check combustion temperature spread |
| Coating spallation, base metal exposed | Coating life reached, or a localised hot streak | Assess against OEM limit; plan for repair or recoat at next outage |
| Crack at a cooling hole or trailing edge | Thermal cycling, frequent starts and stops | Measure and compare to OEM limit; frequency of re-inspection increases |
| Leading edge dents or tears (FOD) | Loose hardware or debris ingestion upstream | Inspect upstream stage or combustor immediately |
| Heavy coking on fuel nozzles or liner | Fuel quality, nozzle wear, or combustion tuning drift | Fuel system and nozzle review, not hot section repair alone |
| Erosion, pitting on compressor blades | Dust ingestion, inlet filtration gaps | Check inlet filtration and, in the north, harmattan-season derating |

## Reporting standard: what a usable borescope report contains

The value of a borescope inspection is destroyed by a report that is a folder of unlabelled photos. A report worth keeping and comparing against next time should contain, at minimum:

- Which ports were opened and which stages were actually viewed, including any that could not be accessed and why
- Labelled images and, where the equipment supports it, measured findings (crack length, coating loss area) rather than descriptive terms alone
- Running hours and starts on the unit at the time of inspection, and the date of the previous borescope or hot gas path inspection for comparison
- A finding-by-finding assessment against the OEM's stated limits for that component and stage, not a generic pass or fail
- A clear recommendation: continue to next scheduled inspection, re-inspect at a shorter interval, or bring forward the next planned outage

Keeping that report against the previous inspection's images, stage by stage, is what turns a single snapshot into a trend, and a trend is what actually justifies moving an outage date. A plant that only has this year's photos, with nothing to compare them to, is one borescope inspection behind where it should be.

If a borescope finding points toward a hot gas path inspection or overhaul rather than another interval of monitoring, that finding is the starting evidence for scoping the work rather than opening the unit blind. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a borescope inspection take?

It depends on the frame and how many ports are opened. A limited check of the combustor and first-stage nozzles can be done in a few hours once the unit is cool and isolated. A full set of ports across the compressor, combustor and turbine section is closer to a full working day, longer again if measured findings need detailed photography.

### Can a borescope inspection be done while the turbine is running?

No. Every borescope port on an industrial gas turbine requires the unit to be stopped, isolated under lockout and tagout, and cooled to a safe handling temperature before the port cover is removed. Anyone offering to inspect a running hot gas path through a port is describing an unsafe practice, not a shortcut.

### Does a borescope inspection replace a hot gas path inspection?

No. A borescope inspection is a visual and, with modern equipment, measurement-based check of component condition through existing access ports. A hot gas path inspection opens the casing and allows hands-on inspection, cleaning, and replacement of hardware. The borescope tells you whether the hot gas path inspection can wait, is due, or should be brought forward.

### What causes most borescope findings on Nigerian sites?

Thermal cycling from frequent starts and trips drives most cracking findings. Dust ingestion, worse during the harmattan season in the north, drives erosion and compressor fouling. Fuel and combustion system issues drive coking and deposit findings. Very few findings on a well-run unit are simply age on their own.

### Who should carry out a borescope inspection?

It should be carried out by personnel qualified on the specific frame, working under a permit to work with the unit confirmed isolated. The findings should then be assessed against the OEM's stated limits for that component, not against a generic figure, because limits differ by frame, stage and finding type.
